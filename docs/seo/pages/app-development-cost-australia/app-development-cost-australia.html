"How much does an app cost?" is the first question every founder asks, and the honest answer is a spread, not a number. Search for app development cost Australia and the guides quote anywhere from $30,000 to $350,000. Those figures are real, but they're what full-service agencies quote for complete builds. Missing from almost every guide is the other end of the scale: what a founder with a real-world budget can actually get built. This page covers both.

The short version

  • Most Australian agencies quote $30,000–$300,000+ for a custom app.
  • A focused first version from a lean local team typically lands at $5,000–$20,000.
  • Complexity, platform choice and design drive most of the price.
  • After launch, expect to spend roughly 10–20% of the build cost every year to keep the app healthy.

A founder who knows which levers move the price — and the next section lists them — can hold a quote near the bottom of its band. A founder who can't, usually won't.

What actually decides the price of your app

Five things move the price more than anything else. Understanding them puts you in control of the quote, not the other way around.

1. Complexity. The number of features matters less than how deeply they interact. A login screen is simple. Login plus paid memberships plus an admin panel plus emails triggered by what users do — that's a system, and each addition touches the others. Complexity is the single biggest cost driver; experienced teams estimate it decides well over half of your total price.

2. Platforms. A native app is built in the specific language of one system (Swift for iPhone, Kotlin for Android). Building natively for both is effectively two projects. A cross-platform app uses one shared codebase (tools like Flutter or React Native) that runs on both — typically 30–40% cheaper than two native builds, which is why most studios, us included, recommend it for a first version.

3. Design. Clean design using standard components might cost $5,000–$15,000. Fully custom design — unique animations, brand-specific layouts — runs $15,000–$40,000 or more. Every extra screen adds wireframing, design, build and testing time.

4. The backend. This is the engine room users never see: the servers, database and APIs (the connections that let apps talk to other systems) that store data and make features work. A simple backend might add $10,000–$20,000. A marketplace backend — payments between strangers, live sync — can easily become the most expensive part of the build.

5. Who builds it, and their rate. Australian agencies typically charge $120–$200 per hour. The industry formula is simple: estimated hours × hourly rate = core cost. A mid-range app might need 600–1,200 hours, which is why agency quotes start where they do. Lean studios and freelancers charge less per hour and, more importantly, quote fewer hours — because they build less.

From experience

Early last year a founder showed me three quotes for the same app: $28,000, $65,000 and $140,000. The expensive one wasn't triple the work — it included a customer web portal, a second app for staff, and a year of support. The cheap one cut testing. When quotes spread that far apart, they're rarely lying. They're building different things. Your job is to make them price the same thing.

App costs by type: simple, mid-range and complex builds

Features have rough price tags of their own. These are typical Australian agency figures for a single feature, built properly:

  • Login and user accounts: $2,000–$8,000
  • Push notifications (messages sent to a user's phone even when the app is closed): $1,500–$5,000
  • Payments (taking card payments through a gateway like Stripe): $3,000–$12,000
  • Maps and GPS tracking: $4,000–$18,000
  • In-app chat: $6,000–$25,000
  • Admin dashboard (the web screen where you manage users and content): $5,000–$20,000
  • AI-powered recommendations: $15,000–$80,000

A simple build — a content app, a basic booking tool, a company app with a handful of screens — combines two or three of these and lands in that $20,000–$50,000 agency band. A mid-range build adds accounts, payments and an admin dashboard: think e-commerce, loyalty programs, service booking. A complex build is where features multiply: marketplaces where buyers pay sellers, delivery apps tracking drivers in real time, social platforms serving video. Each new user type roughly doubles the work, because each needs its own screens, permissions and testing.

Timelines track the price. Simple builds take one to three months, mid-range two to five, and complex platforms six to twelve or more. If a quote promises a marketplace in six weeks, the schedule is fiction or the scope is smaller than you think — either way, ask.

What a first app costs a founder (the honest low end)

Now the part most guides skip. Suppose you're a founder with your own money on the line and something between $5,000 and $20,000 to spend. What can you genuinely get built — not a throwaway demo, but something real people can use?

Done well, that budget buys a minimum viable product (MVP — the smallest version of your app that delivers real value to real users). Within $5,000–$20,000 a lean Australian team can typically build: one platform or a cross-platform build, three to five screens, one core job done properly — a booking flow, a payment, a search — plus a basic way for you to manage the content.

What you give up at this level matters too: no second user type, no custom animations, no real-time anything, and a design that's clean rather than bespoke. That's not a compromise you'll regret. Every successful app you can name launched small, learned from real users, and grew. The founders who get burned are the ones who spend everything building version three before anyone has used version one.

Where you sit inside the $5,000–$20,000 band depends on a few practical choices. Toward the bottom, expect a single-platform build with a standard component kit, one core feature, and you handling your own content through a simple screen. Toward the top, you can add the second platform, payments, a proper admin dashboard and some brand personality in the design. The honest way to think about it: $5,000 proves the idea works with real users; $20,000 makes the first version good enough to charge for.

Remember this line when the quotes start arriving: a $15,000 app in users' hands teaches you more than a $150,000 app in a slide deck.

The costs after launch most guides skip

The quote covers the build. Owning the app is a separate line on the budget, forever. Plan for these from day one:

  • Developer accounts: Apple charges about AU$149 per year to distribute on the App Store; Google Play charges a one-off fee of roughly US$25 (about AU$40).
  • Hosting: the servers your backend runs on typically cost $50–$500 per month, more if traffic spikes.
  • Maintenance: Apple and Google update their operating systems every year, and each update can break things. Budget 10–20% of your build cost annually for updates, fixes and small improvements.
  • Your app's name: worth protecting. A trade mark through IP Australia costs a minimum of $250 and takes at least seven months to register.
  • Marketing: an app nobody finds earns nothing. Even a modest launch budget matters.
  • Third-party services: the maps, SMS and payment tools inside your app charge by usage. They're cheap early and grow with your user base — worth knowing before growth surprises you.

A useful rule of thumb: whatever your build costs, have another 20–30% of that figure available for the first year.

How to read a quote without getting ripped off

A good quote is readable by a non-technical founder. Before you sign anything, check:

  • Line items, not a single number. You should see design, development, testing and project management priced separately.
  • Hours and rate. If you can't see how many hours each part takes and what rate applies, you can't compare quotes fairly.
  • What "done" means. Does the price include app store submission, bug fixes after launch, and training? If it's silent, ask.
  • Who owns the code. You should. The quote or contract should say the intellectual property is yours on final payment.
  • Fixed price vs hourly. Fixed prices protect your budget but invite strict scope limits. Hourly billing is flexible but needs a cap you both agree on.
  • The payment schedule. Deposits are normal; the balance of risk isn't. Never pay everything up front. Payments tied to delivered, working milestones keep both sides honest.

None of these checks takes more than a few minutes, and together they screen out most of the trouble before it starts. The simplest test of all: if a quote shows one number, no breakdown and no mention of testing or who owns the code, ask every question before paying a deposit — not after.

Seven ways to pay less without building a worse app

  1. Build the MVP, not the vision. Launch the smallest useful version. Add features when real users prove they're wanted.
  2. Start on one platform. If your audience skews iPhone, launch there first. Add Android with revenue, not hope.
  3. Go cross-platform. One codebase for both systems typically saves 30–40% over two native builds.
  4. Cut features, not quality. A five-screen app built properly beats a twenty-screen app built badly. Removing scope is the one saving that never costs you later.
  5. Write a clear brief. Vague briefs produce padded quotes. The clearer your scope, the tighter the number — some teams charge $5,000–$15,000 for a formal discovery phase that pays for itself in avoided rework.
  6. Reuse standard parts. Login, payments and notifications have proven off-the-shelf building blocks. Custom-building them is paying a plumber to make his own taps.
  7. Phase the roadmap. Agree version 1 now and put everything else in version 1.1, 1.2 and beyond. You buy time to learn before you buy features.

Your app cost questions, answered

Can I build an app for under $10,000 in Australia?

Yes — if the scope is genuinely small. A focused first version with three to five screens and one core feature is achievable with a lean local team. What's unrealistic under $10,000 is a marketplace, social network or anything with payments flowing between users.

Why do Australian agencies quote $30,000+ when some developers charge far less?

Agency rates reflect Australian wages, full testing, project management and the overhead of a permanent team. Lower quotes usually mean freelancers, offshore teams, or a much smaller scope. None of these is automatically wrong — but compare them on what's actually included.

How long does it take to build an app?

A founder-level first version takes roughly 6–10 weeks. Simple professional builds run 1–3 months, and complex platforms take 6–12 months or more.

Is a no-code builder cheaper?

For a simple internal tool, yes — no-code platforms can get you live for a fraction of a custom build. You trade away custom features, ownership of the underlying product, and easy growth later. Many founders use them to test demand before committing to a custom build.

What will the app cost me every year after launch?

Plan on 10–20% of the build cost per year for maintenance, plus hosting ($50–$500 per month), the Apple developer fee (about AU$149/year) and any marketing.

App budgets reward the informed. You now know the real bands, what moves them, and where a founder can start without overcommitting.
