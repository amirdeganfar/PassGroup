How to pay someone to build an app: the five steps

  1. Write a one-page brief

    Not a spec — a brief. Who the app is for, the one job it must do, the two or three screens that matter, and your budget range. This single page makes every quote that follows comparable, and it flushes out builders who quote before understanding.

  2. Shortlist three builders

    Freelancers, studios and agencies all get quotes from the same brief (more on who's who below). Check apps they shipped alone, talk to one past client, and note who asks good questions — the ones who interrogate your brief are the ones who've been burned before.

  3. Get comparable quotes

    Ask each shortlisted builder for a quote against the same brief: total price, what's included (design? testing? store submission?), payment schedule, and who owns the code. Three quotes on the same page turn a gamble into a decision.

  4. Pay by milestone

    Never everything up front. A deposit to start — commonly somewhere between 20% and half — then payments tied to chunks of finished, demonstrated work. Each payment should buy something you can see and use, not a promise.

  5. Hand over properly

    Before the final payment, you should hold the code, the accounts, and the signed IP assignment. This is the step people skip and regret — the handover checklist below covers what "properly" means.

What you pay for: the quote, deposit and milestones

A proper quote names the work, not just the number: discovery and design, the build itself, testing, store submission, and who pays for what when something changes. Payment schedules vary by builder, but the pattern that protects you is the same everywhere — money follows demonstrated progress. Every milestone payment should trace to something you've seen working on your own phone.

Two habits save the most pain here. First, keep a retention: hold back a meaningful slice — 10–20% is common — until the handover checklist is done. Second, treat change requests as priced events. Scope will move; the question is whether changes get a known price before the work or a surprise after it.

Worth asking every builder, too: what happens if either side stops? A fair contract says what you'd pay for work done to date, and what you'd walk away holding — code, designs, and the rights to them. It's a ten-second question that separates builders who expect to finish from builders who expect to be forgiven.

What you own: code, accounts and IP

Ownership is the part first-timers get wrong most often, and it's bigger than the code. You should own the source code (in a repository you control from day one), the accounts (the Apple and Google developer accounts, the domain, the cloud hosting — all in your name), and the intellectual property. Under Australian law, contractors don't automatically hand you IP rights just because you paid — the default rules can surprise people, and they differ across borders. From experience

The costliest pattern we see is simple: a founder paid 100% up front, the work drifted, and by the time alarms went off there was no milestone left unpaid to negotiate with. The builder held the code, the accounts were in the builder's name, and every request now competed for attention with paying clients. That founder didn't lose money to a scam — just to a structure with no leverage in it. Same project, opposite outcome, when the payments and the paperwork ran in the right order.

What to get in writing before paying anything

Signing something short beats arguing about something unwritten. Before the first payment, tick these off:

  • A contract that says you own the IP and the code, with assignment on payment
  • A written scope — what's in, what's out, what counts as done
  • A payment schedule tied to milestones you accept, with a final retention
  • A change process — new requests get a price before work starts
  • Accounts in your name, or a dated plan to transfer them at handover
  • A stated after-launch arrangement — who fixes bugs, on what terms

None of this is adversarial; good builders sign these terms happily and often provide their own versions. Hesitation is data, though. If a builder resists putting ownership or milestones in writing, that tells you something before your money does.

Paying someone offshore vs here: the same steps, extra checks

Everything above applies wherever your builder lives — the five steps don't change across a border. What changes is the cost of getting them wrong: different legal systems, harder enforcement, and fewer easy ways to verify who you're dealing with. If you're weighing an offshore quote, add two checks: a video call with the actual people who'll write your code (not the salesperson), and a small paid trial before any milestone commitment. The rate might be a third of a local one, but the cost to hire app developers overseas is only cheaper if the structure holds — the savings can be real, and the structure protecting you matters more, not less.
