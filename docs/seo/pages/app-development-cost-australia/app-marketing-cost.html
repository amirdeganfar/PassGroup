The short version

  • Marketing is a separate, ongoing budget — not part of the build quote.
  • A common industry rule of thumb is 12–20% of revenue for marketing; pre-revenue apps should set a fixed dollar amount instead.
  • The cheapest levers are app store optimisation and free channels — do them before buying ads.
  • Track your cost per install from day one so ad spend follows evidence, not hope.

Why marketing is a build cost nobody budgets

Founders budget for the build, the Apple developer account, maybe the first year of hosting. Then launch happens, and the app sits in the store with 14 downloads, 11 of them friends and family. We see it constantly. The build gets every dollar of attention and marketing gets whatever's left, which is usually nothing.

It's understandable. The build is a one-off with a clear quote attached. Marketing is ongoing, fuzzy and easy to postpone. But "build it and they will come" has never been true — the App Store alone lists hundreds of thousands of apps, and every one of them is fighting for attention. A modest, planned marketing budget beats a big, panicked one months later. The same discipline you'd apply to your first-year running budget applies here: know the number before you need it.

The short answer: your first-year app marketing cost

There's no single figure, and anyone who quotes one without knowing your app is guessing. What we can give you is the planning bands we talk through with founders — treating year one as the test-and-learn period:

$0–5kLocal or niche business app — free channels, small ad tests
$5–30kConsumer app building a real install base with paid campaigns
$30k+Funded products chasing fast growth across many channels

Those bands assume you do the free work well (more on that below). They're also spread across the year, not spent in one launch-week burst — a pattern we recommend to every client: test small, measure, then put money behind what works. Once an app earns revenue, the common industry rule of thumb is to reinvest 12–20% of it into marketing, easing toward the lower end as the app becomes established.

Free marketing that actually moves numbers

Before you spend a dollar on ads, exhaust the free stuff. Done properly, it carries many small-business apps a long way:

  • Tell your existing audience. Email list, social accounts, current customers. It's the warmest audience you'll ever have. The business.gov.au guide to social media for business is a free, plain-English starting point.
  • Ask early users for ratings and reviews. Apple confirms ratings and reviews appear in search results and influence how an app ranks — a handful of honest reviews does quiet, compounding work.
  • Show the app in the real world. A two-minute demo at a local networking night converts better than most paid clicks. For a local business app, this is often the whole launch plan.

Paid installs: what users cost per download

Paid ads are the lever people mean when they ask about marketing budgets. The metric that governs all of it is cost per install (CPI) — simply the money an ad campaign spends divided by the number of people who installed the app because of it. Spend $2,000 on ads and get 500 installs, and your CPI is $4.

What that number looks like varies enormously — by country, by app category, and by how well your ads are made. Competitive categories like finance pay far more per install than a casual game. So don't budget against a magic figure; budget against a test. Put a small amount in — a few hundred dollars — run it for two or three weeks, measure the CPI and, more importantly, what those users do next. An install that never opens the app is money burned at any price. When a campaign proves it brings users who stick, that's the moment to scale it.

Store optimisation: the cheap lever

App store optimisation (ASO) is the practice of making your app easier to find inside the app stores' search — the way SEO works for websites. It costs mostly time, which is why we call it the cheap lever. Apple's own App Store search guidance for developers explains what moves rankings: your app's title, subtitle and keywords, plus ratings and how users behave once they find you.

Two details from Apple's guidance are worth knowing as a founder: you get a 100-character keyword field to tell the store what your app relates to, and irrelevant keywords are a common reason apps get rejected. Pick words a real person would type, don't repeat words already in your app name, and skip the broad ones like "app" you'll never win. Few founders do this well — which is exactly why it's a gap worth claiming. It's also small change next to the fees covered in what Apple and Google charge you.

The order that works: ASO and free channels first, small paid tests second, scaled campaigns only when the numbers say so. Reversing that order is how marketing budgets die young.

Marketing budgets by app type

Different apps need very different launch money. Roughly, by type:

  • Local or niche business app (booking, loyalty, internal tool): mostly free channels. Your year-one spend might be a few thousand dollars, often less.
  • Consumer app (fitness, habit, social): needs paid installs to find strangers at scale. Plan a meaningful year-one band, spent in measured steps.
  • Marketplace: you're filling both sides — buyers and sellers — so expect the heavier end, focused on whichever side is harder to get.
  • Game: the most ad-driven category of all, where early CPI discipline decides whether the whole project lives.

Whichever row is yours, put the marketing figure in the budget before you sign a build contract, not after. It changes what you can afford to build — and it pairs with the year-after costs in what app maintenance costs per year to give you the true total. If the full picture feels like a lot, that's the point of finding out now.
