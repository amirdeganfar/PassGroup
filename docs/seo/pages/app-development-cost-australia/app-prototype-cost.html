Prototype vs MVP vs full build: the difference

The three stages get mixed up constantly, and the mix-up costs money. A prototype is a design you can tap through — every screen drawn, flows connected, but nothing underneath. An MVP is a working app with one core job done properly. A full build is the complete product with accounts, integrations and polish. The prototype exists to answer "is this the right app?"; the MVP answers "will people use it?"; the full build answers "can it grow?"

Order matters more than size here. Each stage only makes sense once the one before it has answered its question — you can't MVP an app you haven't designed, and you shouldn't fully build an app nobody has used. Founders who compress the order (skipping straight to a full build) aren't saving time; they're borrowing it at high interest.

The short answer: app prototype cost

In Australia, a professional clickable prototype typically budgets between $5,000 and $15,000, depending on how many screens and flows it covers; a basic wireframe set can land in the low thousands, and lean studios often quote under the agency figure. Three things move you inside the band: screen count (each screen is designed and connected), flow complexity (a booking flow with three user paths is more work than a five-screen linear demo), and how finished the design needs to look — rough-and-tappable is cheaper than investor-grade.

Set against a $30,000–$60,000 MVP, that's a small ticket — which is the point. The app prototype cost is measured in weeks and five figures; the mistake it prevents is measured in months and six.

What a clickable prototype gets you

Hand someone your prototype and three useful things happen, none of which a written idea can do:

  • Real feedback — users tap where you expected and where you didn't; confusion shows up in minutes
  • Better quotes — builders price the same screens instead of three different imaginations, and our guide to getting an app development cost estimate shows how much tighter those numbers get
  • Early credibility — investors and early adopters respond to something they can use, not a slide
  • Cheap changes — a redrawn screen costs a designer's day; a rebuilt feature costs a team's fortnight

How long a prototype takes

Weeks, not months — the whole point is speed to feedback.

Wireframes come first — rough boxes that fix the screens and flows. Those become the clickable version, usually within two to four weeks at a studio. Then you test: five or six people tapping through, saying what confuses them. One or two refine rounds and you're standing at a decision with evidence instead of hope.

Keep the testing loose and honest. Friends and family will be kind; strangers with the problem you're solving will not, and it's their reactions you're paying for. A café table, a link, and ten minutes of watching someone else use your idea will tell you more than another month of thinking about it.

Turning a prototype into an MVP without paying twice

The fear is always "won't I pay for design twice?" — and the honest answer is no, if you keep the same team. The prototype's screens, flows and user feedback flow straight into the MVP's design phase, which is why we count prototype spending as the first instalment of the build, not a separate fee. What does get thrown away is the guesswork: every wrong flow you correct in the prototype is a feature you never paid a developer to build the wrong way. The MVP timeline then runs shorter, because the design decisions are already made.

A prototype is the cheapest place your idea will ever be wrong. Every fix there is a fix you didn't pay a developer to make.

One practical note while you're testing: your idea is worth protecting during these conversations. Business.gov.au's guidance on intellectual property covers what's protected and how, and a simple confidentiality agreement covers the rest for prototype-stage sharing.

When you can skip the prototype

Skip it when the app is a small internal tool, when you're the only user, or when you're copying a screen-for-screen clone of something users already know. Also skip it if your "prototype" would just be a description — in that case, spend the money on a first version instead. For everything else — anything with customers, multiple screens, or investors on the horizon — the prototype earns its keep. We've watched founders change their entire app's direction after one afternoon of watching strangers tap through it. That afternoon costs less than almost any other hour in the whole project.

And if you're weighing the prototype against just "getting a few quotes" instead, remember what a quote can and can't tell you: a quote prices the idea you described, while a prototype tests whether that idea was right in the first place. Price the wrong app correctly and you've still bought the wrong app.
