Ask five developers what a "simple app" costs and you'll get five different numbers. That's not the industry being cagey — it's that simple means different things on each side of the conversation. To you it might mean "an app for my clients to book in". To a developer it means screen count, user types, integrations and how much custom logic hides behind each button. So here's the honest version of the simple app development cost question: in Australia, a genuinely simple app typically runs $30,000–$60,000 from an agency, less from freelancers or no-code tools — and the biggest risk isn't overpaying, it's a "simple" idea quietly growing into a big one. What 'simple' actually means to a developer

Every quote starts from the same inventory, so it's worth knowing how it's counted. A developer reads your idea as:

  • Screens — each one is designed, built and tested
  • User types — customers and staff and managers are separate experiences, not one
  • Logic — calculations, rules and decisions the app makes for you
  • Connections — payments, maps, emails, other software it must talk to
  • Accounts — logins, password resets, profiles (deceptively expensive)

The tell we hear in discovery calls is almost always the word "just". "It's simple — it just sends quotes, takes deposits, and tracks jobs." Everything after "just" is a feature, and each feature multiplies the others. Simple isn't a feeling; it's a low count across those five rows.

Simple app development cost: the price band

Around that band, here's how the market shapes up. A freelancer or two-person studio can often deliver a simple app for roughly half the agency figure — lower overheads, but you carry more of the project management yourself. No-code and app-builder tools can land in the low thousands a year, with real limits on custom logic. And offshore quotes dip lower again, with the usual caveats about rework and management time. What pushes a build back up the band is rarely the first screen — it's the second user type, the third integration, or the feature that "wouldn't be hard".

Two boundary notes keep expectations honest. Under about $10,000, you're generally buying a no-code build or a very thin product — not custom software. And above roughly $80,000–$100,000, whatever you're building probably isn't simple anymore; that's fine, but call it what it is and budget for it like any bigger product.

Five screens, one job: what that looks like

So what does "simple" actually buy you? Picture a jobs app for a small trade business. A simple version holds about five screens and one rule:

  • Home — today's jobs, next appointment, one tap to contact
  • Request a job — pick a service, add a photo, choose a time
  • Confirmation — the booking, the price, what happens next
  • Job history — past work and invoices, no login required
  • Admin screen — you see and manage the incoming list
  • Not included: customer accounts, in-app chat, reviews, loyalty — version two material

That's the shape of an honest simple app: one job done end to end, nothing half-built. It typically takes eight to fourteen weeks to design, build and launch — our guide to how long an MVP takes to build explains that timeline in full.

What quietly turns a simple app into a big one

Cost rarely jumps; it creeps. These are the four quiet growers we see most:

  • A second user type. "Can my staff also log in?" — that's a new role, new permissions, new screens, and every existing screen now needs to check who's looking at it.
  • Accounts. Logins seem basic until you meet password resets, email verification and profile management. Skipping accounts is the single biggest simplifier available.
  • Integrations. Each connection — payments, accounting, SMS — adds build time now and breakage risk later, because those services change without asking you.
  • Custom design. A clean standard look is quick; a unique animated brand experience is a design project sitting inside your app project.

None of these are bad features — they're just not version-one features. Before anything earns a place, run it past the test below.

The one-question test: before adding anything, ask "does this help the one job, or is it a second job?" Second jobs belong in version two — funded by what version one actually earns.

Simple apps still have running costs

Even the leanest app isn't buy-once. Apple's developer account runs about US$99 a year and Google's is a one-off US$25, hosting for a small app typically costs tens to a few hundred dollars a month — our page on whether apps need a server explains why — and you should still budget roughly 15–20% of the build per year for updates and fixes, because iOS and Android keep moving under the app whether you touch it or not. Simple apps are cheaper to run, not free to run. Set that money aside the same way you'd set aside a planned budget for any ongoing commitment, and the running costs stop being a surprise.

How to keep your app simple on purpose

Simplicity survives development only if someone defends it. This checklist is how we defend it with clients:

  • One sentence describes the app's job — printed, agreed, stuck to
  • One user type in version one; roles wait for version two
  • No accounts unless the job truly needs them
  • Standard design components, custom only where the brand earns it
  • Integrations limited to payments and one must-have
  • A written "not in version one" list — signed by you, especially on a bad day

Keep every tick on that list and the simple app development cost stays in its band: roughly $30,000–$60,000 at Australian agency rates, weeks not quarters to launch, and running costs you can name in advance.
