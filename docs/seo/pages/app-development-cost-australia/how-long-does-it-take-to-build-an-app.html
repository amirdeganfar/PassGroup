How long does it take to build an app? The short answer

Time follows scope, and scope means the list of things the app must do. A simple app with a handful of screens sits at the short end. Add user accounts, payments, an admin panel or links to other systems, and each one adds design, building and testing time. Our page on how long an MVP takes covers the smallest version in detail. An MVP is the simplest version of your app that real people can use.

Two apps that sound alike can land far apart. A booking app for a single café is a different job from one that handles many venues, payments and staff logins. That's why the same idea can take eight weeks or eight months depending on what version one includes.

Note that these are typical ranges, not promises. A studio that has seen your type of app before will estimate better than one that hasn't. Ask any studio how they got to their number.

Weeks one to four: discovery and design

Nobody should write code on day one. The first stretch is for deciding what you're building and how it will look. Discovery means workshops and a written scope. Design means sketching each screen, then turning the sketches into a clickable prototype you can tap through on your phone.

This feels slow because nothing "works" yet. But it's the cheapest time in the project. A change on a drawing takes minutes. The same change after the app is built can take days.

One thing speeds this stage up more than anything else: a clear brief. If you arrive knowing who the app is for, the three or four jobs it must do, and an example of an app you like, the first weeks go much faster. If you're still deciding, that's fine, but expect discovery to run longer.

The build: why 8-16 weeks is normal

For a fuller app, the building stage alone often runs 8-16 weeks. Smaller apps get through it faster, which is how a focused app ends up inside 6-12 weeks overall. The build is more than the screens you see. Underneath sit sign-in, a database, the server, links to payment and email services, and the logic that ties them together.

Launch: testing, stores and the review queue

Before launch, the app is tested on real phones, because an app that works on one handset can break on another. Then you submit it to the Apple App Store and Google Play, and both check it first. Apple says its review typically covers at least half of submissions in under 24 hours and 90% in under 48 hours. If a reviewer rejects the app, you fix the issue and resubmit, so we plan a buffer of a week or two.

Rushing costs money: the crash-diary truth

A story from the desk

We once talked to a founder who'd been quoted "three weeks, all in" elsewhere. The app launched on time. Then the crash reports started: it froze on older phones, and the sign-in failed on slow connections. He spent the next two months paying to fix what testing would have caught. He told us later that the rushed version took longer than a properly planned one would have.

Rushing doesn't remove work. It moves it to after launch, where it's more expensive and your first customers see it. Testing is the stage most often squeezed. If a deadline is tight, cut features instead. A smaller app that works beats a bigger one that crashes.

Timeline vs budget: trading one against the other

You can trade time for money, but only a little. Adding more developers to a project doesn't speed it up in a straight line, because the work has steps that depend on each other. Three things do work reliably:

  1. Cut scope

    Launch with the core features and add the rest later. This is the strongest way to save weeks.

  2. Decide fast

    Quick feedback on designs saves more time than extra staff. A design waiting a week for approval is a week lost.

  3. Build in stages

    Release a first version, learn from real users, then build the next piece. Phased app development works this way.

Remember that launch isn't the finish line. Apps need regular updates to stay working as phones change, which has its own yearly cost. We break that down in our guide to app maintenance costs per year.

The takeaway: pick the date that matters, then agree with your studio what version one includes by then. That conversation, not a faster team, is what protects your launch.
