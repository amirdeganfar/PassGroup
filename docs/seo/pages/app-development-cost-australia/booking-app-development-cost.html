Booking app development cost: the short answer

For a single business — a salon, a clinic, a tradie crew — expect a custom booking app to start around $20,000 and comfortably reach $50,000 once reminders, payments and an admin screen are in. Add multiple locations, franchise rules or a customer portal on top, and quotes typically push past $70,000, with complex setups running well into six figures. Timelines usually run two to four months for the simple end. Restaurants are their own story — the booking grid is only one slice — and our guide to restaurant apps covers that bigger picture.

Put that next to the alternative: established booking subscriptions cost anywhere from free (solo operators) to roughly $50 a month, SMS reminders included. At $30 a month, a $40,000 build is the price of more than a century of subscriptions. That's the honest starting point for any build-versus-buy conversation — a build only makes sense when a subscription genuinely can't do the job.

What actually drives the price of a booking app

A booking screen looks trivial: a grid of times, some greyed out. The cost hides in the rules deciding which times a customer can actually pick. Six things reliably blow out a cheap quote:

Cost driverWhat it meansWhy it adds hours
Availability rulesWorking hours, holidays, per-service durations, minimum noticeBehaves like a rules engine, not a settings page
Buffer timeHidden padding for setup, cleaning or travel between jobsChanges which slots can be offered, for every service
Multi-resource bookingA staff member and a room and equipment free at onceEach extra resource multiplies the availability maths
Double-booking raceTwo customers tapping the same slot at the same secondNeeds locking designed in — invisible until a busy day
Calendar syncBookings appearing in staff Google or Outlook calendarsTwo-way sync is a permanent maintenance surface
Cancellations & refundsReleasing slots, cancelling reminders, refund rulesOften costs more to build than the booking flow itself

If a quote prices booking as "a calendar with payments", ask how many of those six rows are included. The answer usually explains the gap between the cheapest and the most expensive quote you're holding — the same gap-finding trick works on an app quote read line by line.

What a bookings-only first version costs

Most businesses that do need a build need far less than they think. A focused first version does one job: customers pick a service, choose a time, pay a deposit or the full amount, and get a confirmation and a reminder. You get a simple admin screen to see the day's bookings and block out time. No customer accounts, no loyalty points, no reviews — those can come later, funded by real usage.

That version is the $20,000–$50,000 band above, and it's the one that pays for itself. Research on appointment reminders consistently finds they cut missed appointments by around a third, and deposits change behaviour in a way a phone call never will. Do your own arithmetic: annual bookings × no-show rate × average job value = money a modest build can claw back. If that number dwarfs the quote, build. If it doesn't, don't.

Custom build or off-the-shelf booking tool?

Run this test before spending anything: trial a subscription tool for a month. If you end up running a spreadsheet alongside it, re-entering bookings by hand, or explaining rules to customers over the phone, you've found the evidence a build is justified — and a spec, written in your own workarounds. If the tool just works, you've saved yourself thirty grand.

Off-the-shelf wins when…

  • You book one person, one service, one hour at a time
  • Your rules are close to standard (hours, breaks, holidays)
  • You're happy for the tool's brand to sit in front of customers
  • The subscription costs less than the no-shows it prevents

A custom build wins when…

  • No tool models your workflow, no matter how you bend it
  • Booking should live inside a product you already own
  • Per-booking marketplace fees now outgrow a one-off build
  • You need to own the customer relationship and data

That last row deserves a caution. Owning your customer data is a real strategic reason — but it's also the reason most often used to justify a build the numbers don't support. And if you do hold customer names, phones and payment details yourself, you take on the responsibilities that come with them, including notifiable data breach obligations under the Privacy Act. That's a running duty, not a line item.

The running costs most owners don't budget for

The build price is the down payment, not the bill. Three ongoing lines surprise owners most:

  • Support and maintenance: budget 15–20% of the build cost per year for fixes, yearly iOS and Android updates, and the breakage that arrives whenever a calendar or payment provider changes something. Our guide to what apps cost per month to maintain walks the full list.
  • Reminders and messages: SMS costs a fraction of a cent per message — trivial at 200 reminders a month, real at 20,000. Email is cheaper but easier to ignore.
  • Payments: every card payment takes a small percentage plus a fixed fee. On deposits and cancellations, make sure your refund rules are built to match what you promise customers.

Hosting for a booking app is modest — booking traffic is small and spiky — so if a quote carries a hefty monthly server figure, ask why.
