The five ways founders pay for builds

1Bootstrapping — your own cash, staged
2Friends, family and angels — the people route
3Grants and the R&D incentive — government money
4Pre-sales — customers first
5Investors — other people's money, other people's say

Most real builds mix two or three of these. A common shape: bootstrap a first version, add a grant or tax offset, then raise from investors to scale what's proven. Each route trades speed against control — the faster the money arrives, the more of the app's future you share. Working out how to get funding for an app therefore starts with an honest question: how much evidence do I have today, and how long can I wait?

Bootstrapping: staging the spend yourself

Bootstrapping means paying in stages from your own pocket. It forces the discipline every other route will demand later: build the smallest thing that sells, prove it with real users, then fund the next slice. Done well, it's the cheapest funding there is, because you only buy what the evidence calls for. Size that first slice with our MVP development cost guide, then stretch it further with the staging tactics in phased app development. The honest limitation: bootstrapping is slow, and some apps — marketplace apps especially — need a critical mass of users before they're useful to anyone. If that's yours, budget for a longer runway or pick a different route. A second trap is the quiet one: staging only saves money if the first stage is chosen well. Cutting scope to hit a number, rather than cutting features to reach a proof point, just delays the same bill.

Friends, family and angels: what they expect

Friends and family fund the person. Angel investors — wealthy individuals who back early companies — fund the upside. Both expect the same three things. Simple terms in writing: a loan agreement or a small equity slice, drafted once, signed by everyone. A realistic budget they can read in one sitting — our realistic app budget guide is the honest template, build plus running costs plus marketing. And honesty about risk: most early app ventures don't return money, and the people closest to you deserve to hear that first, not last. Put the terms in writing even when it feels awkward — it protects the relationship more than the money does.

Grants and the R&D incentive

Government money doesn't dilute you — nobody takes a cut of your app — but it is slow, competitive and usually matched, meaning you spend first and claim or co-fund alongside. Two doors are worth knowing. The R&D tax incentive returns a share of genuine research-and-development spending at tax time; the rules and registration live on the official portal. And grants fund defined stages like commercialisation or export — the federal grants and programs finder shows what's open versus closed. The trap is treating funding as guaranteed. Applications fail, rounds close, and a build that only works "with the grant" doesn't work.

Pre-sales and revenue-first building

The cleanest funding is a customer's. Pre-selling — a paid pilot, a deposit, a founding-member deal — proves demand and funds the build at the same time. It only works if the promise is one you can keep: sell a defined version one, with a date, and build exactly that. Two tips from experience: price the work before you pitch it, and put the delivery terms in writing. Founders who pre-sell before pricing end up apologising; founders who price first end up negotiating from strength. Crowdfunding sits nearby on the spectrum — platforms let many small backers fund the first version — but it rewards products with a story people can rally around, and the campaign itself is a project you must fund and run. Treat it as marketing with a deadline, not passive money.

What investors want to see in your budget

What convinces investors

  • A build number that matches real quotes
  • Running costs and marketing funded, not just the app
  • Milestones the money unlocks, stage by stage
  • Proof that someone will pay for it

What ends conversations

  • A budget that is one number with no tail
  • "We build it and they will come"
  • Numbers that only work if nothing goes wrong

An investor reads a budget the way a builder reads a quote: it tells them whether you've done this before. Bring the three-bucket version — build, run, market — with a stage plan attached, and the meeting changes tone. One more thing separates the two columns above: a stage plan that spends against milestones. Saying "the first $40,000 builds the pilot, and here's what we'll know when it's in ten hands" answers the question every investor is really asking — what happens to my money if this works, and what if it doesn't.

What we see on the ground

A founder we worked with spent three months chasing a grant that never landed — and in that time, built nothing. The reset was simple: stage a lean MVP on their own card, pre-sell five pilot customers from the industry group they already belonged to, then revisit the grant with a working product as evidence. The second application read completely differently, because this time it was backed by proof.
