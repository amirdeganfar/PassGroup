Cheap app development usually buys you one person, part-time, building the smallest version of your idea on the cheapest tools available. Sometimes that's exactly the right call. What does cheap app development actually include?

At the very low end, you're buying hours, not a team. A $3k–$10k build typically covers a freelancer (a self-employed developer you hire directly) or a tiny offshore team, one platform — Android or iPhone, not both — and a simple app: a few screens, a form, a list, maybe payments through an off-the-shelf service. There's rarely a designer, rarely a tester, and no project manager. That's not sneaky; it's what the money buys. The four things cheap quotes skip

When a quote lands at a third of the others, it's usually not that the developer works cheaper. It's that four line items never made it in.

Design. The screens get built by the person coding them. Users notice — clunky apps get deleted, and one-star first reviews are hard to shake.

Testing. Nobody systematically checks the app on different phones, old systems or bad connections. Bugs surface in your users' hands instead.

Documentation and handover. The knowledge of how the app works lives in one person's head. If that person vanishes, the next developer starts from scratch.

Scope clarity. No written list of what you're getting. That cuts both ways: you can't be overcharged for extras you didn't ask for, but you also can't hold anyone to anything. ASIC's Moneysmart site has a blunt rule worth borrowing for quotes that look too good: be suspicious of easy money — there's always a catch.

The $3,500 delivery app

Early last year a founder showed us a signed $3,500 quote for a food-ordering app — payments, menus, the lot. What he got was a website wrapper with a buy button that worked on the developer's phone. Testing? Nobody had opened it on an old Android. The rescue cost him more than double the original build, plus a season of trading without the app he'd sold to three cafés. The quote wasn't dishonest; it was just missing everything that makes software work in the real world.

The real cost of building cheap: rework and rebuilds

Here's the pattern we keep seeing. A cheap build gets to "working app" — for one user, on one phone. Then real users arrive and the cracks show: crashes, slow screens, features that don't quite work together. Fixing them on top of messy code costs more than building cleanly, because every patch risks breaking something else. Eventually you're paying a second team either to rebuild sections or to start again — and a rebuild charges you full price for a second time.

The maths to run before you sign: cheap quote + likely rework + months of delay, versus one properly scoped build. If the first number is close to the second, "cheap" was never cheap.

When a cheap build is still worth it

A cheap build makes sense when…

  • You're testing demand and the app is genuinely simple
  • You'd happily throw it away if the idea flops
  • You own the code and accounts, in writing
  • Nothing sensitive (payments, health data) is involved

Stretch the budget when…

  • The app handles money, bookings or customer records
  • Users will judge your business by it
  • You plan to grow features over years
  • The quote skips testing and documentation entirely

There are also legitimate ways to spend very little without cutting corners — no-code builders, phased builds, or keeping v1 deliberately small. Our round-up of the cheapest ways to build an app covers those honestly, including their limits.

One more honest trade: a cheap build usually ships faster, and speed matters when you're testing an idea. If your plan is "try it for a quarter, then decide", paying full price for version one can be the bigger mistake. Cheap is a strategy when you choose it on purpose — it's only a trap when the quote pretends the trade-offs don't exist.

How to sanity-check a very cheap quote

Ask three questions and listen for specifics. What's not included? A good developer answers with a list — design, testing, third-party fees — before you ask twice. Who owns the code? The answer should be "you, as stated in the contract." What happens if it breaks in month two? A warranty period or a bug-fix window is normal; shrugging isn't. If quotes for the same app range wildly, our page on why app quotes vary so much explains what each band is actually pricing.

Then do one thing most buyers skip: show the cheap quote to the expensive developer. Ask what they'd cut to hit that number. Their answer is the fastest education in app pricing you'll ever get — usually a list of exactly the items this page described, with prices attached.

One rule: never judge a cheap quote on its total alone. Judge what it includes, who owns what it produces, and what a fix would cost if it goes wrong. Cheap app development done deliberately — small scope, owned code, honest limits — is a bargain. Done carelessly, it's a deposit on a rebuild.
