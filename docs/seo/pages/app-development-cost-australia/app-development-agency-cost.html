How you pay varies too, and it changes what the quote means. Fixed price locks the budget but freezes the scope — every change is a new conversation. Time and materials bills actual hours, so the budget flexes with the idea. Longer partnerships often settle on a monthly team rate. None is better; just make sure the quote you're reading and the quote you're comparing it to are priced the same way.

Why agencies cost more than freelancers

An agency hourly rate buys more than coding hours. Behind every project sits work nobody sees until it's missing: design done properly, testing on real devices, project management, and the coordination of several specialists. A freelancer bills you for the hours they work; an agency bills you for the team those hours connect to. Neither model is a rip-off — they're different products, and confusing one for the other is where founders get burned in both directions.

The practical difference shows up on the days things go wrong. Your developer is sick — an agency reassigns. Your app needs a design decision and a backend change at once — an agency runs both. A single freelancer juggles brilliantly, but the juggling is visible in your timeline. That resilience, not fancy offices, is what the premium actually pays for.

The $30k–$300k guides vs founder-budget builds

Those wide "how much does an app cost" ranges mislead founders in two opposite ways. Some founders see $300,000 and delay a project that a $40,000 focused version would have tested perfectly. Others see the low end and expect a full platform for it. The honest read: agencies can start small, but they price by scope — so the question isn't "can I afford an agency?" but "which slice of the app does my budget actually buy?" What agencies include that freelancers can't

Not every agency includes everything, and this is where quotes diverge most. Typically, though, an agency engagement bundles:

  • Discovery and design — scoping the build and designing every screen before code starts, so the quote reflects reality
  • Testing and release — device labs, review rejections handled, app store listings managed
  • Project management — a named person accountable for timeline and scope
  • After-launch support — maintenance retainers and update work from the same team that built it
  • Continuity — documentation, code handover, and staff who can pick up where others left off

That last one matters more than it sounds. An admin panel — the web dashboard where your team manages the app's content — is invisible to users, but someone has to build and maintain it. Agencies include that in scope by default; solo developers sometimes leave it as a surprise. Ask.

Worth saying plainly: none of this makes agencies the right answer for every project. A tight, well-defined tool with a hands-on founder can be a terrible fit for an agency process — and overbuying is its own budget mistake. The point of this page is that you buy the fit deliberately, not by default.

How to compare two agency quotes fairly

Two quotes for "the same app" can legitimately differ by $40,000 — the skill is working out whether they're actually for the same thing. Five steps:

  1. Normalise the scope

    List every feature in each quote. If one includes payments, logins, and an admin panel and the other says "backend integration", you're not comparing prices — you're comparing scope.

  2. Do the hours-times-rate maths

    Hours × rate should roughly equal the total. A quote hiding its hours is harder to challenge when things drift.

  3. Read what's excluded

    Exclusions aren't sneaky — they're honest. But they're money you'll spend later, so count them.

  4. Meet the actual team

    Who designs, who codes, who tests, and who do you call? Resumes beat logos.

  5. Check the after-launch terms

    Who owns the code, what maintenance costs, and how updates are priced. The government's guide to negotiating a contract is a good pre-signing read for exactly these clauses.

Run any agency quote through those five steps and the labels usually swap places. Here's the pattern in miniature: last year we reviewed two quotes for the same booking app — $38,000 and $78,000. The cheap one hadn't priced the admin panel, testing, or launch support. Same app on paper, different apps in truth, and the founders would have discovered that in month two.

Price alone never tells you which quote is better; scope-matched, they finally can. And if a quote can't survive step two — the hours-times-rate check — treat that as your answer.
