One marketplace, three builds (not one app)

The myth to retire: "a marketplace is just a listings app with payments." In practice every two-sided platform is three coordinated builds sharing one backend:

  1. The buyer side — search, listings, profiles, checkout, reviews. This is where most of the engineering goes, because buyers have alternatives and the storefront has to earn the transaction.
  2. The seller side — listing creation, availability or inventory, orders, payout tracking. Smaller, but it decides whether supply shows up at all.
  3. The admin console — moderating listings, resolving disputes, setting commissions, suspending bad actors. The build founders forget, and the one that becomes an emergency at the first counterfeit listing or payout fight.

Budget for two and the third arrives as a change order. The admin console is routinely the most under-scoped line in early quotes — it's not glamorous in a demo, but it's the part that runs the market.

The trust layer: payments, reviews and disputes

Strip the screens away and a marketplace does one thing a normal app never does: it makes a stranger comfortable paying another stranger. That job has a price, and it's the defining cost line of a two-sided build:

  • Split payments and payouts — collect from the buyer, carve out your fee, route the rest to the right seller, survive refunds. Payment providers like Stripe Connect handle the regulated mechanics; the flows around them (when funds release, what triggers a refund, who eats a chargeback) are custom work that typically adds $10,000–$20,000 on its own.
  • Held funds and buyer protection — money sits until the goods arrive or the job is done, with a claims path when something goes wrong.
  • Two-sided reviews — ratings that only unlock after a completed transaction, in both directions, so reputation accumulates and bad actors surface.
  • Identity checks — light for a $20 second-hand item, heavy for accommodation or in-person services, usually billed per check through a third-party API.

None of this photographs well, which is exactly why clone scripts and cheap quotes skip it — and why they fall over the first time a buyer and seller disagree about what was sold.

The chicken-and-egg cost most guides skip

The software is half the bill. The other half is liquidity — enough sellers that buyers find something, enough buyers that sellers keep listing — and no amount of code manufactures it. Every empty marketplace you've ever opened died here, not in the build.

That story is the pattern, not the exception. The cost nobody quotes is the operational spend: seeding supply, moderating listings, answering disputes, and the marketing to keep both sides growing. A planning rule of thumb from operators: expect year one to cost about twice the build once that work is counted — the same start-up maths any new venture carries, which business.gov.au's start-up cost calculator helps you frame.

Do

  • Launch in one niche or one city where you can personally seed both sides
  • Start web-first — buyers arrive from search, sellers manage listings on a laptop
  • Decide who pays your fee early; it changes what gets built
  • Budget the trust layer and the admin console from day one

Don't

  • Build for two cities before one city transacts
  • Ship native apps plus web before you know the model works
  • Buy "an Airbnb clone" and expect the disputes to resolve themselves
  • Spend the liquidity budget on more features instead

How to launch lean: one niche, one city

Three cuts shrink the number without gutting the idea. First, one category: a rentals-only or services-only marketplace is a meaningfully smaller build than a platform trying to be everything, because each transaction type carries its own booking, payout and dispute logic. Second, web-first: one responsive web app instead of web plus two native apps is one build instead of three, skips app-store review, and gets found by search from day one — Airbnb spent its first two years web-only. Third, platform before custom: hosted marketplace tools will run a real two-sided trial for a monthly fee, which is often the cheapest honest test of whether both sides of your market turn up. Our page on whether you can build an app for free covers that ladder in detail.

Custom is the right call once the model proves out — when your fee structure, trust mechanic or workflow doesn't fit any platform, or platform fees at your volume outgrow a build. Many strong marketplaces run that sequence deliberately: rent the software to prove the market, then build to own the economics. And stage the build against a realistic MVP timeline rather than a launch-date fantasy — marketplaces are marathons dressed as sprints.
