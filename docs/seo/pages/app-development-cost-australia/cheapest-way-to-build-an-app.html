The short version

  • No-code puts a working version in users' hands for $0–$100 a month in platform fees plus your time.

What is the cheapest way to build an app?

Three honest paths, cheapest cash outlay first:

  1. Build it yourself with no-code. Drag-and-drop tools let a non-coder assemble a simple app — you pay with evenings instead of dollars. The true cost of no-code app development is platform fees and learning time.
  2. Hire a freelancer on a tight, fixed scope. One screen set, one core job. Rates run lower than agencies, but you manage the project.
  3. Buy a fixed-price first phase from a studio. More than the first two, but you get a team, testing and code you own — see what an MVP costs.

Where cheap builds go wrong

Cheap fails at the scope, not the invoice. The pattern we keep seeing: a founder takes a low quote, then adds "one more screen" every fortnight until the price catches up — with no testing budget left.

Do

  • Sort features into "day-one must" and "later"
  • Ship the smallest thing real users can try
  • Spend on quality for the core flow

Don't

  • Accept a quote with no written scope
  • Trim testing to save money
  • Pay everything before handover

Cut features, not corners

Quality on login, payments and your one core job is the last place to save — bugs there cost users, and the hidden costs of app development follow bad builds everywhere. Features are where cost grows, and cutting them is free. Going alone? Weigh what it costs to make an app by yourself — your hours are money too. business.gov.au's start-up guidance says the same: know the minimum before you spend.

Is no-code always the cheapest way to build an app?

For simple workflow, booking or store apps, usually yes. Custom features, heavy data or connecting to other systems push you back to code quickly.
