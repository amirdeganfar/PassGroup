Ask what a food delivery app costs and you'll get quotes from $20,000 to $300,000 — and unlike most tech price puzzles, both extremes can be correct. It depends entirely on which of three very different products you mean: an ordering app for your restaurant, a marketplace for your suburb, or an Uber Eats-style platform with its own driver fleet. In Australia, the honest budget ranges are roughly $20,000–$50,000 for the first, $50,000–$120,000 for the second, and $120,000–$350,000+ for the third. Three kinds of "food delivery app" (and three prices)

The phrase "food delivery app" hides a fork in the road. Pick your lane before you pick a developer — the prices are not close:

Kind of appWhat you getTypical AU budget
Single-restaurant ordering appYour menu, cart, payments, order alerts for kitchen staff, pickup or your own delivery$20,000–$50,000
Local marketplaceMany restaurants onboard, one ordering experience, you coordinate handover$50,000–$120,000
Full fleet platformRestaurant portal + customer app + driver app + dispatch and live tracking$120,000–$350,000+

Most food businesses actually need the first kind and are being quoted the third. That single mismatch is behind most of the sticker shock in this category.

A quick ownership check. The single-restaurant app suits a venue with a loyal regular crowd that wants to escape third-party commission fees — you're digitising your own orders, not fighting for a suburb. The local marketplace suits someone building a small business in the middle: signing up ten or twenty restaurants in one area and giving customers a single place to order from them. The full fleet platform is a different animal entirely — you're now competing on delivery speed and driver supply, and your budget has to reflect that from day one, not month six.

What drives your food delivery app cost up

Three things separate a $20k build from a $200k one. Fewer user groups is the first: a single-restaurant app serves customers and a kitchen; a marketplace adds restaurant onboarding, commissions and driver management — each new user group is a new app surface to design, build and test. Live tracking is the second: showing a driver's scooter crawl along Hoddle Street in real time means maps, GPS and a backend that never stops updating — industry estimates put maps and tracking around the $11,000 mark on their own, in-app chat near $15,000 — our guide to live map tracking shows exactly where those hours go. Payments and refunds are the third: taking money is one thing (~$8,000 for card payments in a typical build); refunding it, splitting it with restaurants, and paying drivers are what actually cost.

Restaurant-facing features add up quietly too. Menu syncing (so tonight's sold-out special disappears from the app automatically), stock alerts, and promo engines all add hours — budget for them deliberately rather than discovering them mid-quote.

Check two things in any delivery-app quote: who updates the menu (you'll do it weekly), and whether the per-order payment fees are included in their costing. Stale menus and surprise fees are the two complaints we hear most about cheap builds.

The running costs nobody budgets

Delivery is the most operationally hungry app category on this site. Beyond the usual updates and fixes, plan for:

  • Servers and tracking data — typically a few hundred to a couple of thousand dollars a month once live orders flow. We've covered the basics in whether your app needs its own server.
  • Per-order fees — card gateways and SMS alerts each take a cut or a per-message fee, so every order carries a small cost. On thin restaurant margins, that's a pricing decision, not a rounding error.
  • Support and refunds — wrong orders, late deliveries, missing items. Someone has to answer the phone, even (especially) at 8pm Saturday.
  • Updates and fixes — budget 10–20% of the build per year; our monthly maintenance cost guide breaks the line items down.

If your version employs or contracts drivers, the working arrangements matter as much as the code — the government's contractor obligations guide is the right place to start before you launch a fleet.

Start with ordering, add tracking later

The cheapest food delivery app that's still genuinely useful is an ordering app, not a tracking app. Version one keeps the parts that make money and skips the parts that look impressive:

  • Full menu with photos and prices — the thing customers actually open the app for
  • Cart, checkout and card payments — where the revenue happens
  • Order alerts to the kitchen — a simple ticket screen beats a printed docket
  • Pickup or delivery toggle — let customers choose; you sort the driving
  • Basic promo codes — the cheapest loyalty tool there is
  • Live driver tracking, route optimisation, chat — version two material, funded by version one's success

A build like that typically takes one to three months — and if the numbers need a second opinion before you commit, our guide to how professionals cost an app shows what a defensible quote looks like.

Those fees scale with success, which makes them easy to plan for and easy to forget. Picture a modest month of 1,000 orders: card fees of a couple of per cent on a $40 order, plus a few cents of SMS alerts per customer, plus your hosting line — call it a low three-figure bill before anyone fixes a bug or answers a support call. It's rarely the app that runs out of money at launch; it's the month the orders arrive. Build these lines into your pricing from the first quote, not after the first invoice.

Ordering first, tracking later: version one needs the menu, the cart and the payments. The live map is a feature for when the orders are already flowing.

One pattern from our own clients says this better than any table. A Melbourne takeaway we worked with had been quoted a "DoorDash-grade" platform — driver app, live map, the works — and nearly shelved the whole idea when the quote landed. Their real problem was simpler: phone orders were drowning the kitchen at peak. The app they actually needed was the ordering version above. It paid for itself inside the first year on fewer missed orders alone, and the tracking features became a later decision, made with money they'd earned rather than money they'd borrowed. Start with the pain your business actually has — the software should follow it, not lead it.
