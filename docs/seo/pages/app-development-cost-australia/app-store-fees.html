US$25 buys a lifetime pass to Google Play. Apple charges US$99 — every year — for the same door. Set against a build, app store fees are pocket money. But they have more moving parts than founders expect: two kinds of fees, two commission systems, and rules about what the stores may take a cut of. Here's the whole picture in plain words, so nothing on the store side of your budget is a surprise.

There are two fees every app pays before anyone earns a cent: the developer account that lets you publish, and — if you sell digital goods — a commission on sales through the stores. Both are small and predictable compared to the real app development costs in Australia, which is exactly why they're worth five minutes of planning now. The pattern I see in draft budgets is the same every time: the build is costed to the dollar, and the store side is a shrug. The two fees every app pays: accounts

To publish an app, you open a developer account with each store. Think of it as a shop licence: it identifies you, lets you upload apps, and stays active as long as you keep paying. The account is also where the paperwork lives — tax details, banking details for your payouts, the legal agreements you sign before selling anything. The two stores run opposite models — Apple bills yearly, Google bills once — and for a new founder the side-by-side looks like this:

Apple App Store

  • US$99 per year to stay enrolled
  • 15% commission for most small developers (Small Business Program, up to US$1M a year)
  • Standard rate is 30% beyond that

Google Play

  • US$25 once, when you sign up
  • 15% of the first US$1M you earn each year
  • 30% only on earnings above US$1M

Apple's annual fee, Google's one-off fee

Apple's Developer Program membership renews at US$99 every year — a rent, not a purchase. (There's a separate US$299 enterprise tier, but it exists only for companies distributing private apps to their own staff, not public apps.) Google charges a one-off registration fee, currently US$25 — pay once and the account is yours to keep. One habit matters more than the rates: set both accounts up in your own business name and diarise Apple's renewal. A lapsed account can pull an app offline, and reinstating it takes longer than paying ever does.

The commission: when the stores take a cut of your money

Sell digital goods or services inside your app — unlocks, credits, premium subscriptions — and the store that processed the payment keeps a share. Sell nothing inside the app, and the commission is zero. Google publishes its service fee schedule in full, and the tiers reward small developers on both platforms:

FeeApple App StoreGoogle Play
Developer accountUS$99 per yearUS$25 once
Commission — small developers15% under the Small Business Program (up to US$1M a year)15% of the first US$1M each year
Commission — beyond that30% standard rate30% standard rate
Auto-renewing subscriptions30% in year one, then 15% — or 15% from day one under the Small Business Program15% regardless of earnings

Apple's Small Business Program is opt-in and free: if your apps earned US$1 million or less last year, you get the 15% rate, and you re-qualify the year after if you dip below again. Google's tier applies to everyone by default.

What does the commission feel like in practice? If your app sells a $10 in-app subscription through the stores, roughly $8.50 lands with you at the 15% rate — and the store's payment processing, tax paperwork and customer billing support are part of what the cut buys. Where founders get caught is modelling revenue at 100% and costs at 0%. Model payouts at 85% from the start and the commission simply becomes arithmetic rather than a shock.

Google reports that 99% of developers who pay a service fee are eligible for 15% or less. Unless your app is already a proven earner, budget on 15% — not the scary 30% headline.

In-app purchase rules in plain words

What the stores may charge for comes down to one distinction. Selling digital things — features, coins, content, subscriptions — must go through the store's payment system, commission included. Selling physical things — a takeaway order, a booking fee, a shirt — can run through your own payment setup, and the stores take no cut of it. In general terms: if the buyer consumes it on the phone, the store wants its share; if it turns up at the door, it doesn't. That's why a rides app pays no commission on rides but does on its premium subscription, and why a shopping app can take card payments directly. Your developer will confirm where each part of your app sits, but now you know why the two categories are treated differently.

Other small store costs (reviews, updates, rejections)

Good news: the expensive-sounding parts are free. Every app review costs nothing. Every update you ship costs nothing to submit. Even a rejection costs no fee — its price is the delay while you fix what the reviewers flagged and requeue. Reviews typically take days, not weeks, though timing varies and no approval is ever certain — one more reason launch dates need breathing room. The only real store-side costs are the accounts, the commission on digital sales, and your patience during review.

Budgeting for app store fees in year one

Put the store side of the budget on a calendar and it becomes almost boring:

  • Month 0 — open both developer accounts: US$99 for Apple, US$25 for Google
  • Build phase — sign the paid-apps agreement so launch day isn't delayed by paperwork
  • Launch — commission starts only when your digital sales do
  • Every year after — Apple's US$99 renews; check your commission tier once a year as revenue grows

Store fees are set in US dollars and billed in local currency, so the exact Australian dollar figures move with exchange rates — another reason to keep them as bands in your budget rather than fixed amounts. Two more habits keep the store side tidy: check your commission tier once a year as revenue grows, and have your accountant confirm how store payouts and GST fit your setup before the first sales flow. Store fees are the most predictable slice of your app budget — which is more than can be said for the build itself.
