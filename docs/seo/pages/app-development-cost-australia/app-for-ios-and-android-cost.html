You've got one idea, two kinds of phone, and one budget. So how much does it cost to build an app for iOS and Android? Usually less than double the price of one app, and often closer to one and a half times. It depends on how you build it: two separate native apps, or one cross-platform app that runs on both.

The short version

  • A focused app from a lean Australian studio typically runs $5,000–$20,000 for one platform.
  • Both platforms cost more, but design and the server side are shared, so it's rarely a straight doubling.
  • Store fees are small: US$99 a year for Apple, a one-off US$25 for Google.
  • If money is tight, launch on one platform and add the other once users prove the idea.

The short answer: one app or two?

Start by noticing that "an app for both" can mean two different things. It can be two native apps, one built for iPhone and one for Android, each with its own code. Or it can be one cross-platform app, built once and published to both stores. Quotes differ a lot depending on which one the studio assumes, so always ask.

$5k–$20kone platform, lean studio
$7k–$25kboth, one cross-platform build
$10k–$35kboth, two native builds

These are rough budgeting guides for a focused first app. More features, custom design or tricky integrations push every number up, and agency quotes typically land in the $30,000–$60,000 range, as we explain in what affects app development cost.

So when someone asks how much does it cost to build an app for iOS and Android, the honest reply is another question: which build style, and how big is the first version? Pin those two down and the price range narrows fast. Skip them, and two quotes for the "same" app can differ by thousands.

Two native apps: what doubles and what doesn't

Building two native apps doubles the programming, because each app is written separately. Testing grows too, since each one needs its own checks on its own phones. Those are the parts that nearly double.

Other parts don't. You design the idea once, plan the features once, and build one server behind the apps to store accounts and data. Project management is shared. That's why two native apps tend to cost around one and a half times a single app, not twice, though every project is different.

A quick example makes it concrete. Say a studio quotes $15,000 for a booking app on iPhone only. The Android version isn't another $15,000. The design, the booking rules and the server already exist, so the second build mostly covers new screens, Android-specific fixes and testing. Expect it to land well below the first price, though not near zero. Ask for that second-platform figure in writing, so it doesn't surprise you later.

Cross-platform: one codebase, both stores

A cross-platform tool such as Flutter or React Native lets developers write the app once. A codebase is simply the app's instruction set, so one codebase means one thing to build, test and fix. Most business apps (bookings, shops, member apps) run well this way. Our simple app cost guide shows what a basic version typically costs.

This is usually the cheapest way to be on both stores. The catch is apps that rely heavily on phone hardware. Games, augmented reality and complex animation can cost more, because developers have to write extra phone-specific code.

Maintenance follows the same logic. Two native apps need two sets of updates whenever Apple or Google changes something, while one cross-platform app needs one. That adds up over the years, so compare yearly running costs, not only the build price. Our page on yearly app maintenance costs gives typical figures.

Starting on one platform and adding the other

You don't have to launch everywhere on day one. Many founders build for one platform first, learn from real users, then add the second. It spreads the cost and cuts risk. We cover the idea in building your app in phases.

What founders often get wrong

People pick the platform they use themselves. A better test is where your customers are. A tradies' booking app aimed at business owners can lean one way, while a student app leans the other. Ask ten likely customers which phone they carry. That takes an afternoon and can save thousands.

The trade-off: going second means a second round of testing and store setup, so a later Android or iPhone version isn't free. Budget for it, but spend it only after the first version has proved itself.

Store fees and testing costs per platform

The store accounts are the cheapest part of your budget. Apple's Developer Program costs US$99 per year, and Google Play's registration fee is a one-off US$25. Our guide to app store fees covers commissions as well.

One habit saves headaches: open both store accounts in your own business name, not your developer's. That keeps ownership with you and avoids a messy handover later.

Testing is the platform cost people forget. Android runs on thousands of phone models, so it needs more device checks. iPhone has fewer models but strict store review. Plan for both, and see what app testing costs for what's usually included in a quote.

Don't forget the quiet costs on both sides. Each platform has its own screen sizes, its own store rules and its own review process, and each can send your app back for a fix before it goes live. How to decide without guessing

Work through these steps before you ask for quotes:

  1. Pin down your audience

    Ask likely customers which phone they use. Let that, not your own phone, set the first platform.

  2. Set a ceiling

    Decide the most you'll spend on version one. If both platforms don't fit, go one-platform.

  3. Request both quotes

    Ask for cross-platform and native on the same feature list so the prices are comparable.

  4. Plan the second platform

    If you launch on one, pencil in when and what it will cost.

Ask every studio one question: "Is this one app or two?" Many quotes that look cheap are one platform only.
