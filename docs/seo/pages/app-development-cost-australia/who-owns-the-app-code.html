"I paid for it, so I own it." Mostly true — and the space between "mostly" and "always" is exactly where founders lose their apps. Ask who owns the app code and the honest answer is: whoever the contract says. If the contract is silent, Australian law defaults to the developer, not you. That default is cheap to fix before you sign and expensive to fix afterwards. Ownership is the first thing we settle with clients at Pass Group. We're a Melbourne app studio, and our view is simple: a build you don't own isn't an asset, it's a rental. The default that surprises founders: in Australia, IP created by a contractor belongs to the contractor unless your contract says otherwise — IP Australia states the rule directly.

Who owns the app code by default in Australia?

Start with what the law says before any contract. IP Australia's guidance is straightforward: the creator of intellectual property usually owns it, and work created by a contractor — which is what an external app developer is, even one you pay handsomely — stays the contractor's property unless a contract transfers it. Employers own what their employees build, but your studio isn't your employee. Buying the code outright, or having it assigned to you, has to be written down. Going offshore adds one more layer: with offshore app development the contract crosses jurisdictions, so that written assignment matters even more.

"Code" isn't one thing, either. The source code is the human-readable program your developers edit — the original. What runs on phones is a compiled version of it. Owning the compiled app without the source is owning a photo of a painting: real, but not editable. That distinction is the whole game at handover.

Three things to own in writing: code, design, IP

Ownership splits into three buckets. Founders who get all three in writing are the ones who can change developers, launch in new countries or raise funding without drama:

  • Source code and repositories — every line written for your app, plus the scripts that build it
  • Design assets — wireframes, interface designs, icons and logos
  • The IP itself — the legal assignment of copyright, plus anything patentable and the trademark in your app's name

Watch for the carve-out: studios often keep their own pre-built libraries (login systems, payment wrappers) and licence them to you. That's normal and fair. The test is whether your app still runs and can be maintained if that licence ever stops. Make the proposal answer that question before signing.

One trap hides in the word "IP" itself. Copyright in the code is automatic and assignable by contract, but a trademark — the legal protection for your app's name and logo — is registered separately, and registration is its own process. Likewise, if your app does something genuinely novel, patent territory is a conversation with a professional, not a clause. A good contract assigns what exists today; a great one also says who owns improvements made later. Ask for both.

Accounts in your name: the store, hosting, repositories

Code is only half the ownership story. The other half is accounts, and it's the half that bites. The Apple App Store Connect and Google Play Console accounts — the developer dashboards each store gives you — should be registered to your company email, with you as the account holder. Same for hosting (the servers your app runs on) and the repository, which is where the code lives; GitHub is the common one. If any of these sit in the developer's personal accounts, "your" app is technically theirs. The fix costs nothing at kickoff and can cost everything later.

The same rule reaches the quiet accounts: analytics (the dashboard showing how people use your app), payment gateways (the service taking your customers' money), push-notification services, and the AI or mapping services the app calls. Each holds a piece of your product, and each has a login. List them all — service, address, who holds the login — and keep the list with your contract. It takes an hour at kickoff and saves a week of archaeology at handover.

The handover checklist to demand on day one

Whatever the contract says, this is what "you own it" should look like in practice:

  • Source code and full change history pushed to a repository you control
  • Store accounts in your name, with the app's releases visible from your login
  • Hosting and cloud services billed to your card, in your account
  • Design source files exported and delivered, not just screenshots
  • Documentation — how to build, deploy and run the app, written for a stranger
  • IP assignment signed, dated and delivered with the final invoice

Two details hide in that list. "Change history" matters because the next developer needs to see how the code grew, not just where it ended. And "the right entity" on the assignment matters because the company that signed your contract isn't always the company that wrote the code — the signature has to come from whoever actually owns the work.

What happens to ownership if you part ways

From experience

A founder came to us mid-build, developer gone quiet, and the first question wasn't cost — it was access. The code lived in a repository under the developer's personal account, the store account was the developer's, and there was no assignment clause. Every step of the recovery became a negotiation, because the invoices had never mentioned IP at all. The app survived; the founder paid for the same ground twice. The pattern we now tell every client: access first, assignment in writing, then money changes hands.

If a build has already gone sideways, our guide on what to do when your app developer disappears walks the recovery path. And when the relationship simply ends — a change of scope, a change of team — a clean handover runs on the same checklist either way.

Four questions that catch the answer early

Do ask, before you sign

  • Who owns the repository while we build — and who owns it at the end?
  • Which parts of my app come from your existing libraries, and how are they licensed?
  • When is the IP assignment signed and delivered?

Don't accept

  • "We'll sort ownership out at the end"
  • A final invoice due before the assignment is signed
  • Store and code accounts you've never seen or logged into

Nail those questions and the ownership answer settles itself — in your favour, in writing, before money moves. That's the difference between owning an app and renting one.
