How much does it cost to make an app by yourself: the short answer

  • Short version
  • Prototype on free tools: often under $100 in cash.
  • Ship to the app stores: add US$25 once (Google) and US$99 a year (Apple).
  • The real cost: your hours — easily a few hundred for a first build.
  • Production quality with paid help: typically $15,000–$40,000.

That spread surprises people, so it helps to separate the three different "costs" hiding inside one question: what you pay in fees, what you pay in time, and what you pay when DIY stops being enough. Each has its own section below.

One reframe before the numbers. When people ask how much does it cost to make an app by yourself, they usually picture the tools — subscriptions, accounts, gear. In practice the tools are the cheapest line on the page. What the question really means for most founders is: can I get this idea into the world without paying a team? And the honest answer is yes — for the prototype. That's the version these numbers cover first, because it's the version DIY is genuinely good at.

The direct costs: accounts, tools, hosting

Good news first: the software you need to start is free. Flutter and React Native (two free, open-source toolkits for building mobile apps) cost nothing. The editors you write code in — Xcode for iPhones, Android Studio for Android — are free downloads too. The unavoidable fees are the app stores' own door charges:

Those figures are planning guides, not promises — but they're the honest shape of a first solo build. The trick every founder should use: value your hours at your own rate. If you earn $60,000 a year, your hour is worth about $30. Three hundred hours is $9,000 of you — unpaid, and usually spent at night.

That doesn't make DIY wrong. If you're building skills you'll keep, or the app is a genuine side project, those hours are an investment, not a loss. The mistake is spending them without counting them.

Free tools that get you surprisingly far

A pattern we see

A founder we advised built a booking prototype for her dog-grooming side business over one summer — Flutter, a free backend tier, and a $20 domain. Total cash: under $100. She then chose not to push it further herself, because her prototype had done its job: proving people would book. Knowing where to stop is the skill that saved her money.

The free stack that got her there is available to anyone:

  • Flutter or React Native — free toolkits that let one codebase run on both iPhone and Android.
  • Xcode and Android Studio — the free editors you build and test in.
  • Firebase or Supabase — services that handle logins and data, with free tiers for early builds.
  • Figma — free for sketching your screens before you build them.

Where DIY builds hit the wall

Most solo builds don't fail at the coding — they fail at the last 10%. App-store review rejects broken or incomplete apps. Security needs doing properly, because one leak can end trust. And every year iOS and Android change under you, so an app that ships "finished" needs upkeep from day one. None of this is hidden, but it's easy to underestimate while you're excited about screens.

The second wall is speed. A prototype you tinker with on weekends is fine; a business that depends on the app can't wait for its founder's spare Saturdays. Every week between "it works on my phone" and "it works for customers" is a week someone else can serve that market first. That deadline pressure — not the coding difficulty — is what pushes most working founders to hand over. Mixing DIY and paid help: a common path

The path that works for many founders is a hand-off, not a choice. Build the prototype yourself for pocket money, prove the idea, then pay for the parts where experience matters — the production build, the security, the maintenance. A freelance developer typically charges $80–$150 an hour here, landing simple builds in the $15,000–$40,000 range; our guide to freelance app developer costs has the detail. Whatever you spend, keep the IP question straight: if contractors write code, who owns the IP needs to be in writing.

Two things make the hand-off cheaper, and both happen before you hire anyone. First, a working prototype answers the questions that usually burn budget — the endless early meetings about what the app should do are already done. Second, your own hours teach you the vocabulary: scope, releases, edge cases. Founders who've built once read quotes faster and get quoted straighter. The DIY stage pays for itself even when the app it produced never ships.

The short version: making an app by yourself costs a few hundred dollars in direct fees, several hundred hours of your time, and — if the idea works — a five-figure production build when you're ready. Spend the cheap part first, and buy experience only where it pays.
