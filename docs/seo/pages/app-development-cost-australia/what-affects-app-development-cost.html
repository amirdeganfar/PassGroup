Why do two quotes for the same app idea land $40,000 apart? Because "the same idea" rarely is — each quote is reading a different set of assumptions behind it. If you want to understand what affects app development cost, the honest answer is that seven things decide almost everything: scope, accounts, integrations, platforms, design, who builds it, and what happens after launch. 1. How many screens and features you need

Every screen is designed, built and tested; every feature behind a screen multiplies the work. Five screens with one core job is a different product from fifteen screens with booking, payments and reviews — even if both fit the same elevator pitch. This is why experienced teams price from a screen-and-feature list, not from the idea's name. Even small-seeming extras — features like push notifications — each add their own build-and-test hours to that list. When you compare quotes, count what's actually listed; our app development cost breakdown shows how the same app fans out into line items.

2. Logins, accounts and permissions

Accounts look like one checkbox and behave like a subsystem. Logins bring password resets, email verification, profile management and (once staff join in) roles and permissions — every screen must now check who's looking. The cheapest simplification available to most founders is skipping accounts entirely in version one, or keeping a single lightweight login. Every user type you add — customer, staff member, manager — is effectively another app hidden inside yours.

It's also the factor clients underestimate most often. We've watched a "quick login" grow into two weeks of work more than once, and it's rarely the login itself that costs — it's everything that has to now know about the person on the other side of the screen. Budget accounts honestly or design them out early; half-adding them midway is the most expensive path of the three.

3. Integrations with other systems

An integration connects your app to software you don't own: payments, maps, SMS, accounting, identity checks. Each connection is built once and maintained forever, because the provider on the other end changes their side without asking you. Two or three carefully chosen integrations are normal; ten on a first version is a standing tax on your future. Payments plus one must-have is a common, defensible starting set.

4. Which platforms you build for

Native apps are built separately for iOS and Android; cross-platform tools write one codebase that runs on both, typically saving a meaningful slice of the budget. The trade-off is control: cutting-edge phone features and peak performance still favour native. For a first version, one platform — or cross-platform with a plan — is the usual answer. What not to do is delay the choice until mid-build; it reshapes everything downstream.

A practical middle path many founders miss: build for the platform your first hundred users actually carry. A tradie-heavy audience might mean Android first; a design-agency audience might mean iOS. Choosing one platform for version one doesn't lock the second one out — it funds it, because you'll learn what version two should fix before paying to support two codebases.

5. Design depth: template vs crafted

Design sits on a spectrum. At one end, standard components — clean, fast, and what most successful first apps actually use. At the other, a crafted brand experience with custom animation and illustration, which is a design project living inside your app project. Neither is wrong, but quoting one while imagining the other is how budgets break. Say which end you mean, out loud, before the quote is written.

6. Who builds it and where they are

The same scope prices very differently depending on who holds the keyboard: freelancers, lean local studios, established agencies, offshore teams. Rates and overheads differ by several-fold, and so does what you're buying — project management, testing discipline, continuity when someone leaves. The pattern we see most in quotes that go wrong isn't greed; it's scope written loosely enough that two builders honestly imagined different products.

So compare builders on process, not just price: ask who actually tests the app, who owns the code when it's done, and what happens to the quote when something turns out harder than expected. A slightly dearer quote that answers those three questions in writing is usually the cheaper one by the end.

7. What happens after launch

The quote covers the launch; the app then keeps living. Budget roughly 10–20% of the build cost per year for maintenance, plus hosting and the app-store developer fees — our page on app maintenance cost per month runs the numbers. Business.gov.au suggests planning ongoing costs the same way you'd plan start-up costs for any business: write them down before they arrive, not after.

Before you accept any quote: ask the builder to state, in writing, which of the seven factors above they assumed. Two builders who agree on all seven will usually agree on price.

Bring this list to your next quote meeting

  • A screen-and-feature list, even a rough one
  • Who logs in — and who doesn't, in version one
  • The fewest integrations that still prove the idea
  • Your platform preference, or a stated "recommend one"
  • Template-first or crafted design — chosen, not assumed
  • A twelve-month budget line for hosting and maintenance
