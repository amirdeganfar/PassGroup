Why apps never really 'finish'

An app is not a document you save and shelve. It runs on other people's phones, inside rules written by Apple and Google, talking to servers that change too. When any of those move, your app has to move with them. Three forces guarantee it:

  • Phones change. Both Apple and Google ship a major operating system update most years. Apps must be re-tested and often re-fitted for each one.
  • Users find things. Real people tap in ways no tester predicted. Every live app carries a steady trickle of small bugs.
  • Businesses change. Prices, products, and staff all shift after launch, and the app has to keep up.

I can usually tell which clients planned for this and which didn't within a month of launch. The ones who didn't treat the first repair bill as a betrayal; the ones who did just send the bug report and move on. Same app, same fix — completely different experience.

Small updates vs new features: different price tags

"Update" covers wildly different jobs, and they're priced differently. Before you budget, sort the work into these buckets:

Type of updateTypical AUD costWhat it involves
Bug fixA few hundred–$2,000Find the fault, fix it, test, release
Small tweak (text, colours, one screen)$1,000–$5,000Design touch, code, store review
Yearly phone-update pass$3,000–$10,000Re-test and re-fit for the new iOS and Android releases
New feature (payments, logins, chat)$5,000–$30,000+Design, build, test, release — plus backend work

Those bands assume an Australian studio or freelancer at local rates. The cost to add features to an app is the line that moves most — a real feature touches design, code, and the server behind it, which is why it can cost ten times a bug fix.

The maths behind every band is the same: hours multiplied by the hourly rate. A bug fix might take two to four hours of a developer's time once it's already diagnosed. A new feature like payments needs a designer, a developer, a tester, and a slice of the server changed too — easily 50 to 150 hours before anyone touches a store listing. Same tradesperson, ten times the hours, ten times the invoice.

There's also a fixed cost most people miss: every update, even a one-line fix, passes through Apple's or Google's review before it reaches users. That review is free in dollars but real in time — usually a day or two, occasionally longer if the store flags something. It's one more reason bundling small changes into a single release saves money on both sides of the ledger.

The annual OS-update tax every app pays

Every September and every August-to-October stretch, Apple and Google release their big yearly updates. New phone software means new rules, new screen sizes, and sometimes new permissions. Every published app — yours included — gets re-tested against it. Think of it as a standing appointment, roughly like rego for a car:

  • September–October: the major iOS and Android releases drop. Budget the compatibility pass — commonly $3,000–$10,000 for a small app.
  • Twice a year or so: app store guideline changes, especially around privacy and permissions, need small code updates.
  • All year: user-reported bugs get triaged and fixed in small monthly batches.
  • Every year or two: certificates and software libraries used by the app need renewing before they expire.

Skip that appointment and the app doesn't die overnight — it decays. What happens if you stop paying for maintenance is its own cautionary tale: phone updates, expired certificates, and unfixed bugs usually break an abandoned app within a year.

So, how much does it cost to update an app each year?

Add the buckets together and a small app typically needs a few thousand dollars a year in quiet years, and noticeably more once it's busy. A common planning rule is to budget roughly 10–20% of the build cost per year for upkeep — so a $50,000 app carries an update budget of about $5,000–$10,000 annually. That yearly figure lines up with what founders see on a monthly rhythm too: our guide to what maintenance costs per month shows the same bands split into monthly amounts.

Budget updates as a standing line, not a one-off: quiet year, a few thousand dollars; active app with new features, a tenth or more of the build cost, every year.

Who owns the code decides who can update it

Here's the sneaky cost multiplier: an app can only be updated by someone with the code. If your freelancer or studio kept the source code and the server logins, every update goes through them — at whatever price they name. If you hold the code, any developer can quote the work. IP Australia's guide to who owns IP covers how this works under Australian law, and it's worth ten minutes before you sign anything. Ownership is the difference between shopping your updates around and being locked into one supplier forever.

Budgeting updates from day one

The cheapest update is the one you planned for. Three habits keep the bill predictable:

  1. Set the line at build time. Decide before launch — even a rough 10–20% figure — so the first maintenance invoice is a plan coming true, not a shock.
  2. Bundle small changes. Batch your tweaks into a release every few months instead of paying for a store review per tiny fix.
  3. Buy the boring stuff early. The yearly phone-update pass and certificate renewals are non-negotiable; new features can wait for evidence they'll pay off.

One founder habit worth copying: keep a running wish-list, then pick one item per quarter. It caps the spend, spreads the risk, and — a pleasant side effect — forces every feature to earn its place against the others on the list.

None of this makes updates free — nothing does. It makes them boring, and boring is exactly what you want from a budget line you'll pay for as long as the app is alive.
