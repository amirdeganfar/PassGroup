Which is the safer way to pay for an app: one fixed number, or a bill for the hours worked? Neither is safer by default. Fixed price vs time and materials is really a choice about who carries the risk when the plan changes. Fixed price puts most of that risk on the studio. Time and materials (you pay for the hours and costs as they happen) puts it on you. This page shows how each one works, when it wins, and the trap hiding in each. Fixed price buys certainty when the scope is clear. Time and materials buys flexibility when it isn't. Most healthy projects use a mix of both.

The two payment models in plain words

Fixed price means the studio agrees to deliver a defined list of features for an agreed total. If the work takes longer than they guessed, that's their problem. If you want something that isn't on the list, that's a change request, and it's priced separately.

Time and materials means you pay an hourly or daily rate for the time spent, plus any direct costs such as paid services. The studio gives you an estimate, but the final bill follows the real effort. Business.gov.au describes the same split for any contractor: contracts for labour or skills usually pay an hourly or daily rate, while contracts for a set result usually pay a fixed fee.

Fixed price vs time and materials, side by side

Fixed price

  • Total agreed before work starts
  • Easy to budget and approve
  • Changes are priced as extras
  • Studio adds a buffer for unknowns

Time and materials

  • You pay for effort actually spent
  • Scope can shift as you learn
  • Final cost is an estimate, not a promise
  • You see progress and spend as you go

Notice the buffer in the fixed column. A studio quoting a fixed price must guess every unknown, and a careful one adds a cushion for the guesses that go wrong. You're paying for certainty, so a fixed quote often looks a little higher than the matching hourly estimate. That isn't a trick. It's the price of the studio carrying the risk.

When fixed price wins (and its one trap)

Fixed price works best when you know exactly what you want. A small app with a clear feature list, a design already signed off, and no tricky integrations is a good fit. Your accountant will like it too, because the number is easy to approve and easy to plan cash flow around.

The trap: a fixed price only protects you against the scope you wrote down. Anything vague gets argued about later. If "user accounts" could mean a simple email login or a full profile system, you and the studio may each assume a different one. Fix the trap by agreeing the feature list in plain words before you sign. Our guide to reading an app development quote shows which lines to check.

When time and materials wins (and its one trap)

Time and materials suits projects where the answer isn't known yet. Think of an app that depends on a new idea, a tricky third-party system, or feedback from early users. You'd rather learn and adjust than lock in a plan that may be wrong in week four.

The trap: without limits, the bill can drift. An open-ended hourly deal rewards neither side for finishing. Fix it with guard rails: a weekly or fortnightly report on hours used, a monthly spending cap that needs your sign-off to exceed, and a clear review point at each stage.

From our side of the desk

A founder once asked us for a fixed price on an app with "a few integrations." We couldn't price it honestly because we hadn't seen two of the systems. We suggested a small paid discovery phase on time and materials, then a fixed price for the build. The fixed number came out lower than the cautious one we'd have given on day one, because the unknowns were gone.

The hybrid most studios actually use

Many studios split the job. Early work, where the unknowns live, is billed on time and materials with a cap. Once the plan is clear, the build is quoted at a fixed price. You get certainty where it's real and flexibility where it's needed.

This also fits a staged build. If you launch a small first version and add features later, each stage can be priced on its own. Our page on phased app development explains how that works and why it keeps risk small. A typical focused first build runs roughly 6-12 weeks, so a stage is short enough to price with confidence.

Questions to ask before choosing either

Whichever model a studio offers, these questions show whether the deal is a fair one. Ask them in writing and keep the answers with your contract.

  • What exactly is in the price? Ask for the feature list, the number of design revisions, and what testing covers.
  • How are changes priced? Look for a clear rate or process, not "we'll sort it out."
  • What happens if it runs over? On fixed price, the studio should wear it. On hourly, there should be a cap or a check-in.
  • How will I see progress? Regular demos beat a big reveal at the end.
  • When do I pay? Payments should follow delivered work, not the calendar.

That last question deserves its own look, because the model you pick shapes how and when you hand over money. We'll cover that in a separate page on deposits and milestones.

The honest summary: don't hunt for the "cheaper" model. Pick the one that matches how much you already know. If your scope is solid, fixed price keeps things calm. If it isn't, spend a little on time and materials first, then fix the rest.
