Most small businesses don't need a $150,000 app. They need one job done well — bookings taken, orders placed, or a paper form replaced — and priced honestly. So what is the app development cost for a small business in Australia? A focused first version typically lands between $30,000 and $60,000, a fuller customer app runs $60,000–$150,000, and internal tools often come in under both. What separates the quotes isn't the industry you're in — it's how many jobs the app tries to do. Here's how the numbers break down, what pushes them up, and where small businesses most often overspend. A customer app vs an internal tool: what each costs

"We need an app" means two very different things, and they're priced differently:

Customer-facing app

  • Polished design — it is your brand
  • Accounts, payments, notifications
  • App-store presence and review cycles
  • Marketing budget to get it downloaded
  • Typically the $60,000–$150,000 band

Internal tool

  • Function beats polish — staff just need it to work
  • Often a web app, no app store at all
  • Logins limited to your team
  • Replaces admin hours you're already paying for
  • Often lands under $50,000

The internal tool is the quiet overachiever of small business software. It rarely makes headlines, but it pays for itself in saved admin hours within the year — and it's usually the better first project, because you learn how your business actually behaves in software before you put anything in front of customers.

What pushes a small business app up in price

Four levers move almost every quote:

  • User types. Every role — customer, staff, manager — is its own designed and tested experience. Three roles roughly means three apps' worth of screens.
  • Integrations. Talking to your accounting software, payment gateway or CRM adds build time now and maintenance later, because those systems keep changing underneath you.
  • Custom design. A clean, template-based interface is a fraction of a fully custom, animated one. First versions rarely need the custom end.
  • Feature count. The obvious one. Every screen, button and workflow is hours, and "just one more small feature" is the most expensive sentence in software.
The pattern we see most: it's rarely the app that busts a small business budget — it's the second, third and fourth jobs the app picks up along the way.

Before committing serious money, pressure-test the idea the way you would any new product: who uses it, what it saves or earns, and how you'll know in six months whether it worked. An app is a product decision, not a marketing checkbox.

What a focused first version looks like

The builds that succeed start narrow. If you're commissioning your first app, this sequence has worked for dozens of small businesses:

  1. Write the one job

    One sentence: "Customers book and pay for a service without calling us." If it takes three sentences, cut two.

  2. List what's out — on purpose

    Accounts, loyalty, reviews, dashboards: name what version one won't do. This list saves more money than any negotiation.

  3. Get a scoped, line-by-line quote

    Features in, features out, hours and rate shown. Our guide to getting an app cost estimate shows you what a healthy quote reveals.

  4. Launch, watch, then spend again

    Real usage tells you what version two should be. Build phase two from evidence, not the planning whiteboard.

How narrow is narrow? A café ordering app where customers pick items, pay and show a confirmation: three core screens plus a simple admin list. A tradie's quoting tool that turns a checklist into a PDF with pricing: one form, one output. These are the $30,000–$60,000 builds — and they feel bigger than they sound once they're in customers' hands, because they do the one job completely instead of five jobs badly.

Cheaper alternatives worth checking first

Before you spend five figures, spend an afternoon on the alternatives — one of them often does the job:

Subscriptions first: booking tools, ordering platforms and loyalty apps can be rented for the price of a few coffees a week. Build when the rental genuinely can't model how you work — you'll know within a month of trialling one, because you'll be running a spreadsheet alongside it.

  • No-code app builders — template-driven mobile apps you configure yourself; fine for content, menus and simple loyalty, limited when you need custom logic.
  • A really good website — many "app ideas" are really a mobile-first website with payments. Cheaper to build, nothing to download, found by Google.
  • Off-the-shelf plus automation — forms, spreadsheets and a Zapier-style connector can prototype the workflow before you commit real money.

Running costs a small business should expect

The build is the down payment. Ongoing, expect roughly 15–20% of the build cost per year for maintenance — updates for each year's iOS and Android releases, bug fixes, security patches — plus developer-account fees (about US$99 a year for Apple, a one-off US$25 for Google) and hosting that typically runs tens to a few hundred dollars a month for a small app. Our breakdown of app maintenance cost per month puts real numbers on each line.

One warning while we're on running costs: that maintenance line is the first thing owners cut when the budget tightens, and it's the most expensive cut you can make. Apple and Google ship a major OS update every year, and an unmaintained app slowly breaks against them until the stores pull it. If ongoing cost is the worry, build smaller — don't skip the upkeep.
