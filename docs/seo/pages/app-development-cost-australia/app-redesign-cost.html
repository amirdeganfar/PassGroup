Redesigning an app is either repainting the house or rewiring it — and the price difference is enormous. The honest answer on app redesign cost is that a fresh visual skin on healthy code might run to thousands of dollars, while a redesign of a structurally sick app quietly becomes a rebuild with a decorator's invoice. The trick is knowing which job you're actually buying before you sign.

This page gives you the three job types, the budget bands, and the questions that keep the quote honest. We've sat on both sides of redesign quotes at Pass Group — happy to sanity-check yours free. For the wider money picture, start with our guide to app development costs in Australia.

What does an app redesign cost actually cover?

Three different jobs hide under the one word "redesign", and they price nothing alike:

Skinvisual refresh — new colours, fonts, screens; code untouched
Structurenew flows and layout — screens rebuilt, logic partly rewired
Rebuildnew foundation — redesign plus a code base that can't be saved

Most quotes fail here: a founder says "redesign" meaning a skin job, and the developer hears a structural one — or worse, says nothing and prices the middle. Naming the job is step one.

Redesign vs rebuild: different jobs, different bills

Typical Australian budget bands for small-to-mid apps — treat them as planning ranges, because the code underneath moves every number:

JobTypical band (AUD)When it's the right call
Visual refreshroughly $5k–$15kUsers find the app dated or confusing, but it's stable and features fit fine
Structural redesignroughly $15k–$40kFlows work badly, screens get rebuilt, some logic changes
Full rebuildroughly $20k–$100k+The code itself is the problem — every fix breaks something else

A visual refresh is design work plus careful re-skinning — new styles and screens applied to existing, working code. A structural redesign rethinks how users move through the app: new navigation, merged screens, changed journeys. A rebuild starts over. And if your app is genuinely broken rather than dated, be honest with yourself: a redesign won't fix failing code, and it will cost you a rebuild anyway.

The refresh that wasn't one

A gym owner sent us a redesign brief last spring: "new screens, same everything underneath." Our audit found the last build had stored members' passwords in plain text — a detail invisible in any screenshot. What he'd called a refresh had to be quoted as a security rebuild first, design second. He was annoyed for a day and grateful ever since: no design makes an unsafe app worth looking at. The lesson sticks — the audit writes the job description, not the mood board.

The code underneath decides the price

Two apps can look identical in screenshots and price $8k apart to redesign. What decides it is the code — and the quality of what the last developer left behind. Modern, clean, documented code takes new designs quickly. Old, undocumented or messy code means every new screen is dug out by hand, and the quote has to price the digging. That's why a redesign quote without a code audit is a guess in a nice font. Ask for the audit first — a short, priced review of what you have. Budget for it even if it stings; it's the difference between a quote and a lucky dip. Ownership matters here too: if you don't control the code yet, settle that before redesigning anything — see our page on who owns the app code.

Timeframes follow the same logic. A refresh on healthy code is typically a few weeks of design work plus careful application. A structural redesign runs longer because rebuilt screens must be retested end to end — a changed checkout flow, for instance, touches payments, emails and reporting. Anyone promising a full structural redesign in a fortnight is pricing exactly that testing out.

Rule of thumb: redesign the outside when the inside works. If the quote must also fix crashes, slow screens and broken features, you're buying a rescue wearing a redesign's clothes — price it as one.

Redesigns that pay for themselves

When is a redesign worth the money? When the app is losing users to something fixable. The pattern we see pay off: users download, struggle through the first few screens, and quietly leave — the ratings say the app is "confusing" or "hard to use", not "broken". A redesign aimed at that first-run journey targets the leak directly. The paying-for-themselves kind also tends to be the smallest kind: rethink the two or three screens every user touches, not the whole app. Our guide to the cost of maintaining an app each year covers the regular polish budget that keeps a redesigned app from going stale again.

It fails in a predictable way too: redesigning for taste rather than for users. If nobody has told you the app is hard to use — if the reviews are fine and usage is steady — a redesign is decoration, and decoration rarely returns its cost. Spend the budget on the thing users actually complain about, or pocket it.

And sometimes the answer is neither refresh nor redesign: a small, well-planned update can do the work. Our page on what it costs to update an app covers that lighter path.

Getting an app redesign quoted properly

Before you accept any number, run this checklist:

  • Audit first. A developer who quotes without reading the code is guessing at your job
  • Name the job. Skin, structure or rebuild — get the same words on both sides
  • See the screens early. Wireframes (rough screen sketches) before build, so changes are cheap
  • Keep data where it is. Users, accounts and orders should survive the redesign untouched
  • Plan the rollout. Ask how the new version reaches existing users without a bad surprise

One last guard: redesign sales pitches run on pressure — "brand refresh, sign this quarter" — and pressure is a poor advisor. ASIC's Moneysmart page on avoiding sales pressure is written for exactly that moment: slow down, compare, decide on facts. And get the design work quoted as its own line, separate from any rebuild work the audit uncovers. You want the option to approve each half on its own merits.
