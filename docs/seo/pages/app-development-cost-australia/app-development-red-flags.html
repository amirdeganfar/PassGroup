What do a one-line quote, a 100% upfront invoice and a guaranteed App Store approval have in common? All three are app development red flags — and each one is priced in the only currency that matters: what it costs you when it goes wrong. Most horror stories we've cleaned up after had warning signs sitting in plain sight, in the very first email.

So this page ranks the flags the way a rescue bill ranks them — most expensive first. If you're at the start of the buying process, read it alongside our guide to app development costs in Australia so you know what a fair quote looks like. Seven app development red flags, ranked by what they cost you

  • No IP or source-code clause. The contract doesn't say the code and accounts are yours. Cost if ignored: potentially the whole app — this is the flag behind the biggest rebuilds we see.
  • One line for everything. "App development – $20k." Cost if ignored: no way to prove what you paid for, so every dispute is your word against theirs.
  • Payment front-loaded. Half or more due before a line of code works. Cost if ignored: your leverage dies with the final instalment.
  • Guaranteed store approval. Nobody outside Apple or Google can guarantee it. Cost if ignored: weeks of "it's the store's fault" instead of fixes.
  • No discovery phase. A quote arrives within hours, before anyone has asked what the app should actually do — the default speed at the cheapest end of app development. Cost if ignored: scope arguments by week two.
  • No testing or warranty. Nothing about QA (quality assurance — the structured testing of the app) or a bug-fix window after launch. Cost if ignored: you pay twice for the same features.
  • Pushy urgency. "Sign this week, price goes up Monday." Cost if ignored: decisions made on pressure, not information.

Scam-prevention experts make the same point about any big purchase: be suspicious of offers that feel too easy. Moneysmart's guidance on protecting yourself from scams starts with exactly that instinct — slow down, check who you're dealing with. A developer who resents being checked isn't a red flag; it's a flag on a flag.

The quote that doubled by email

A founder sent us a one-line quote — "app development, $18,000" — and asked what we thought. We asked the developer a simple question: which features does that cover? By that afternoon the price had doubled, now with a feature list attached. Nothing shady was proven; the first number was simply a foot in the door. That's the pattern with most flags on this page: they show up in the first email, when walking away is still free.

Red flag one, up close: "app development – $20k"

A proper quote splits into blocks — planning, design, build, testing, launch support — because that's how the work actually happens. One line hides all of it. Which features? Both platforms? Who designs the screens? You can't compare it to another quote, you can't hold anyone to it, and every conversation after signing starts from zero. If a quote reads like a receipt for a car service, send it back and ask for the split. Our guide to reading an app development quote shows what a good breakdown looks like line by line.

Red flag two: no IP or source-code clause

Intellectual property (IP) means who legally owns what gets built — the code, the designs, the store accounts. In Australia, if the contract is silent, you may not own what you paid for. The fix is one paragraph naming you as the owner, with the code delivered to a repository (a shared code vault) you control. Our page on who owns the app code covers the exact wording to insist on. A developer who won't put it in writing has told you something — believe them.

This flag is also the quiet one. Everything works while the developer is happy and reachable. It only prices itself the day you need a second developer, the relationship sours, or the original team simply moves on — and by then the question "whose code is this?" costs real money to answer.

Red flag three: payment terms that front-load everything

Deposit and milestones should track delivered work: a deposit to start, then instalments against demos you can actually use. Terms like 50% upfront and 50% at "delivery" (with delivery undefined) mean you've paid most of the bill before you've seen anything work — and recovering money from a developer mid-project is a legal fight you don't want.

Red flag four: promised store approval

Apple and Google approve or reject every app on their own terms; no studio controls that. What a good developer does is prepare properly — the platform rules, the privacy details — and stand by your submission if the store pushes back. That's a commitment you can hold. A guarantee is a sentence you can't. Our guide to the app development contract lists the promises that are safe to accept in writing, and the ones that never should be.

The same logic applies to guarantees of "top rankings", "viral growth" or a date for break-even. Nobody can promise outcomes that depend on your market. What a developer can promise — in writing — is their own work: features delivered, testing done, defects fixed within a warranty window. Judge quotes on promises like those, and the flags stop looking subtle.

Fastest check of all: read the exclusions. Every honest quote lists what's not included. A quote with no exclusions isn't generous — it's incomplete.

What a clean quote looks like instead

Walk away when…

  • The quote is one line with no breakdown
  • Ownership of code and accounts is never mentioned
  • More than a modest deposit is due upfront
  • Store approval is "guaranteed" in writing

Expect from a good quote…

  • Costs split into planning, design, build, testing
  • A written IP clause naming you as owner
  • Milestone payments against working demos
  • Exclusions stated plainly, before you sign

For the negotiation itself, business.gov.au's advice on negotiating a contract with a supplier applies neatly: learn who you're dealing with before you sign. Ten minutes of checking — portfolio, reviews, a past client — outranks any promise in an email.
