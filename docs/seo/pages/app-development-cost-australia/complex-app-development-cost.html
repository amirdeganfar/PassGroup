The short answer: what $150,000+ actually buys

At Australian agency rates, genuinely complex apps — think multi-sided platforms, apps that move money, or products that replace software a whole company runs on — typically budget from $150,000 and well past $500,000. Industry surveys of app pricing land in the same territory: enterprise-grade builds are regularly quoted from $150,000 to $500,000+, and complex builds commonly run six months to a year or more before launch. What that money buys is breadth: separate experiences for customers, staff and admins; connections to payments, banking and logistics systems; and engineering that survives those connections changing under you.

The textbook example is an Uber-style platform — driver apps, rider apps, live tracking, payments in and out. We break that exact app down line by line in how much it costs to build an app like Uber. The contrast with a simple app, which typically sits between $30,000 and $60,000, is the whole story: complexity multiplies, it doesn't add. Two user types isn't twice the work of one — it's twice the screens, plus the permission layer that checks who's looking, plus the admin tools to manage both. Each addition reaches into everything already built, which is why the last 20% of scope often costs more than the first 50%.

Integrations: the biggest price driver in complex builds

An integration is a connection to software you don't own — payments, maps, identity checks, accounting, SMS. Each one is built, tested and then maintained, because the provider changes their side without asking you. A complex app usually carries several of them, plus real-time features (live tracking, chat, instant updates), which need infrastructure that's on and fast around the clock. This is the section of a quote that quietly doubles.

From the studio

A founder came to us with a quote for a booking platform: eleven integrations on the first version, including two payment providers and a loyalty engine. A competitor had shipped with three connections and 40% of the features. The competitor was charging customers six weeks earlier — and by the time our founder's build launched, two of his eleven integrations had already changed versions.

Real-time features push this further. Live tracking, chat and instant updates all need a server side that's on and fast around the clock — infrastructure that has to be built, monitored and scaled. That's the part of a complex quote that never really switches off after launch either; it just becomes your hosting and maintenance bill.

Every integration is a promise to maintain a connection someone else controls. In complex builds, that promise is the price.

Security, compliance and audits: the quiet line items

Once your app holds other people's personal information at scale, you carry obligations that don't exist for a simple app. In Australia, if the Privacy Act covers your organisation and a data breach involving personal information is likely to result in serious harm, you must notify the affected people and the OAIC under the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme. Building to that standard means encryption, access controls, admin tooling, audit logs and usually independent security testing — real hours, budgeted as their own line items.

Add anything regulated — health data, payments, financial advice — and the bar rises again. Business.gov.au's cyber security guidance for business is a sensible pre-read before you sign anything. None of this is optional padding; it's the difference between an app that can hold customer data and one that legally shouldn't.

A complex app is a small team working for months: a project manager, a designer, two to four developers, a tester and usually a server-side specialist. At typical Australian agency hourly rates, that team math is how you arrive at $150,000+ honestly — there's no single expensive item, just many months of skilled people. It's also why complex builds rarely come in under their quote: the team is already the minimum viable size for the scope.

Timelines stretch for the same reason. Six to twelve months isn't slowness — it's the sequence. Design has to be signed off before screens are built, server-side work has to run in parallel with the apps that talk to it, and testing can't start until real features exist. Every integration adds a step where someone else's system has to be understood, connected and re-tested when it changes. Compress that sequence and you don't save money; you find the bugs in production.

When the complex app development cost is justified (and when it's not)

Some ideas can't be built small. Others can, and the founder who admits it first saves a year and six figures.

Worth the money

  • Two-sided platforms where users pay each other through the app
  • Replacing a legacy system staff use all day, every day
  • Handling regulated data (health, finance, identification)
  • Real-time features that are the product's promise

Not yet justified

  • Proving whether anyone wants the idea at all
  • One user type doing one job — that's a simple app
  • "We'll need it eventually" features on version one
  • Custom design polish before the first user has logged in

Complexity is a decision, not a default. If a feature isn't load-bearing for your first hundred users, it's version-two scope wearing a version-one disguise.

Cutting scope, not corners: getting the result for less

The reliable way down from $150,000+ isn't a cheaper team — it's a narrower first version. Ship the one workflow that proves the idea, with one user type and the fewest connections you can defend. Then let real usage, not guesswork, choose what version two contains.
