Mobile app development cost in Australia: the short answer

Three tiers cover almost every project. A simple app — a few screens, one login style, no server — sits at the bottom of the range. A mid-tier app with accounts, card payments and an admin web page (a private site where your staff manage content and users) sits in the middle. Complex builds — marketplaces, health data, connections to big government or hospital systems — top the range. That's the mobile app development cost in Australia in one paragraph.

Two honest caveats. First, the tiers overlap: a disciplined small studio can land a mid-tier app near the bottom of the agency band. Second, most quotes assume one platform — add the second iPhone-and-Android pair and the number moves again. Which brings us to the "why".

Why two 'simple' mobile apps can differ by $20,000

The tell I look for in a "simple app" brief is the word just. Just a login. Just a map. Just card payments. Each "just" adds a week or three. The biggest hidden driver is the backend: the part of your app that lives on a server instead of the phone — accounts, saved data, the rules that decide what each user sees. An app that works entirely on the device is far cheaper than one that needs that machinery.

Sign-up flows show how fast it compounds. Email and password is the cheap version. "Sign in with Google" means connecting to Google's systems (an API — a way for two programs to talk), extra security checks, and extra testing. Add an admin portal for staff and the effort quietly doubles. None of these features is wrong — they're just never free, and two apps that look identical in a demo can differ by $20,000 behind the screens.

What iPhone and Android each add to the bill

If you build native — meaning each version is written separately in Apple's and Google's own programming languages — you build, test and update two apps. The front-end work roughly doubles, and every fix lands twice. Testing grows too: dozens of iPhone models and a shelf of Android brands all show your app slightly differently. Store accounts add a little: around US$99 a year for Apple's developer program and a one-off US$25 for Google Play.

A common compromise: start on one platform, whichever holds most of your customers. You can add the second platform once the idea has proven itself. Paying to build and maintain two platforms for an unproven version one is the most common early budget leak we see.

Native vs cross-platform in cost terms

Cross-platform frameworks — ready-made toolkits like Flutter or React Native — let developers write one set of code (one codebase) that runs on both iPhone and Android. Industry guides typically put the saving at 30–40% compared with two separate native builds, and routine fixes get cheaper too. The trade-off is fit, not just price:

Native (two builds)

  • Best for heavy camera, GPS or performance work
  • Fastest, smoothest feel on each device
  • Two codebases: higher build and upkeep cost

Cross-platform (one build)

  • Best for most version-one founder apps
  • Typically 30–40% cheaper than two native builds
  • One codebase: fixes and updates land everywhere at once

For most founder apps we build cross-platform first and go native only when a feature truly demands it. Ask any builder which they'd choose for your feature list, and why.

Where founder budgets fit when agencies quote $30k+

Most agencies quote $30,000–$300,000 because their model is a production team: strategy, design, engineering, compliance. If your budget is $5,000–$20,000, the honest answer isn't a discount — it's smaller scope. One platform first. Fewer integrations. A version one that proves the idea before it wows anyone.

A small budget doesn't buy a lesser app. It buys a smaller version one — and a real-world answer before you've spent the agency money.

Most founders we meet don't need convincing that $200,000 buys a great app. They need someone to say out loud what $15,000 buys. So here it is: a real app, on one platform, doing one thing well, with room left to fix what real users teach you. There are no promises in software — a small build can still flop.
