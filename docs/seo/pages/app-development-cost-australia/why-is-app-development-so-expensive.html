Why is app development so expensive? The honest one-paragraph answer

Because "build an app" isn't one job — it's a small team of jobs happening at once, on a product that must work with zero second chances. Software that ships broken gets deleted, not recalled, so the price includes the thinking, testing and rework that make it not break. Add that custom software is hand-made from nothing every time — there's no factory — and that it must keep working after launch while phones and operating systems move underneath it. That's the expense. Not gold-plating: engineering, multiplied by care.

You're paying several specialists, not one person

A working app is the product of at least five overlapping crafts. A designer decides what screens exist and how they behave. Developers build the app itself and the backend — the invisible server side that stores data and enforces rules. Someone handles integrations, the connections to payment gateways, maps or messaging services. A QA tester breaks it on purpose before your users can. And a project manager keeps the whole thing pointed at your budget. Each craft has its own years of training, and the cheap versions of each are the most expensive words in software.

Hourly rates explain part of the bill too. Australian senior developers commonly sit around the $120–$180 mark, and even at those rates a mid-sized build runs hundreds of hours per person. Multiply one modest team by one modest timeline and five figures arrive before anyone's added a feature. Software is never "done": the maintenance truth

The price on the quote buys version one. But an app is more like a car than a chair — it needs servicing for as long as it runs. Phones get new operating systems every year; payment providers change their rules; security holes get found and need patching. Plan on roughly 10–20% of the build cost per year to keep an app healthy, plus hosting. Skip it and the app doesn't freeze in place — it quietly rots until a screen stops working on a new phone and the reviews say so.

Why the same feature costs different amounts in different apps

"Add reviews" can be a two-day job in one app and a five-week job in another. The difference is what sits underneath. A review feature bolted onto a simple app is a form and a list. The same feature in a marketplace with payments, profiles and moderation is a small legal system: who can review whom, what gets hidden, what gets appealed, what happens to a seller's rating when a review is removed. Each rule needs building, testing and maintaining.

Complexity compounds the same way inside the build. Every integration is another service that can fail; every user type adds its own permissions; every clever feature adds test cases that multiply with everything already there. That's why two apps that look identical in a store can have been utterly different projects to make. Our page on why complex apps cost more follows that multiplication in detail.

Where money genuinely gets wasted (and by whom)

An honest answer needs the uncomfortable paragraph too: some of the expense is waste, and founders cause as much of it as studios. The big three. Feature sprawl — adding "one small thing" every week until the build doubles; each addition is a design, a test cycle and a re-release, not a line item. Premium work in invisible places — custom animation on a settings screen nobody opens. Skipping the thinking — paying for weeks of code before anyone showed the prototype to a real user, then paying again to change it.

Studios waste money too, usually through over-quoting for risk they haven't asked you about, or senior hours spent on jobs a good off-the-shelf tool already solved. The fix on both sides is the same: written scope, staged builds, and decisions based on evidence rather than enthusiasm. Founders who bring that discipline to the table get leaner quotes — not because the rates drop, but because there's less fog to price.

App development is expensive because it's a team of specialists building something unrecallable from nothing, then keeping it alive in a moving world. The waste isn't in the rates — it's in the fog.

Why some apps cost $5k and some cost $300k

Both numbers are real, and neither is a lie. A $5,000–$20,000 build is a narrow first version: one platform, one core job, standard parts, small admin view — the lean tier our page on what a lean MVP costs breaks down. The $300,000 build is the same idea with the dial turned all the way up: both platforms, multiple user types, integrations, custom design, compliance work and the architecture to survive success. The expensive app isn't a scam relative to the cheap one — it's a different product. The skill is knowing which one your idea needs right now, and paying for exactly that.
