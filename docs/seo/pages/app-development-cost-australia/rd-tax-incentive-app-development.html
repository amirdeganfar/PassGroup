What the R&D tax incentive is, in plain words

The R&D Tax Incentive is a long-running Australian government program that hands back part of what you spend on eligible research and development. It isn't a grant you apply for before you build — it's a tax offset you claim after the work, through your company's annual tax return. Two government bodies share the job: the Department of Industry, Science and Resources registers your R&D activities, and the Australian Taxation Office processes the claim. The offset is worth up to $150 million in eligible spend, which is a number most app projects will never meet — the point is the program is real, legislated, and bigger than startup folklore suggests. What it's worth: the current rates

The offset size depends on your company's aggregated turnover — combined turnover of you and any connected entities:

+18.5%premium above your company tax rate, refundable, for turnover under $20 million
+8.5%premium, non-refundable, for larger companies with R&D spend up to 2% of total expenditure
+16.5%premium, non-refundable, when R&D spend runs above 2% of total expenditure

The ATO's rates page sets out the detail. For a typical early-stage startup — under $20 million turnover — the offset is refundable, which means cash back even if the company owes no tax. If your company pays the 25% base rate and spends $100,000 on eligible R&D, the offset is 43.5% of that: about $43,500 back. Your own tax rate changes the maths, so treat that as an illustration, not a promise.

Which app builds qualify (and which don't)

This is where most of the thinking happens. The program funds core R&D activities: experiments that generate new knowledge, where you genuinely can't know the outcome in advance and can only find it through systematic work — a hypothesis, a test, an observation, a conclusion. The test is not "was the app hard to build?" It's "did we not know how to do this until we experimented?"

Likely to qualify

  • Testing a new way to process, match or compress data when no known method fits
  • Experiments with novel hardware or sensor integration where the result is genuinely unknown
  • Prototypes built to answer a technical question — with the results written down

Not eligible on their own

  • Standard apps built with known tools, patterns and APIs — even if well executed
  • Routine features: logins, payments, screens, bug fixes, design polish
  • Work where the outcome was already known when you started

Activities directly supporting a qualifying experiment — running the environment, collecting the data — can also count. Activities the program excludes (they're listed on the government pages) never do. And the claimant must be a company — incorporated in Australia, or in some cases a foreign company with the right Australian tax residence; your tax agent confirms which category fits. A sole trader or a partnership on its own can't claim.

What you can claim: development spend basics

Claims are built from eligible R&D expenditure — in practice, the salaries of your own staff for time genuinely spent on the experiments, payments to contractors who run activities for you, and materials or consumables the work burns through. Each category carries conditions and exclusions, so don't treat a whole build invoice as claimable by default. Two hard edges catch founders: you need at least $20,000 of eligible R&D expenditure in the income year (waived if a registered research service provider runs the work), and only the experimental share of a project counts — not the routine build around it. Records to keep from day one

You must self-assess eligibility, and the department or the ATO can review your claim years later — records are what the claim is actually made of. Keep, contemporaneously: experiment notes (the hypothesis, what you tried, what you measured, what you concluded), timesheets that separate R&D hours from routine build hours, and invoices that tie spend to activities. Reconstructing this after the fact is expensive and rarely convincing. Write it as you go; the notes can be scrappy as long as they're honest and dated.

How the claim process works with your tax agent

Realistically, a registered tax agent (or an R&D tax specialist) should run this process with you — the deadline and the paperwork both have teeth:

  1. Self-assess eligibility

    Confirm the company structure, the activities and the spend fit the program's rules before promising anything.

  2. Register the activities

    Lodge the registration with the department within 10 months of the end of the income year in which the work happened. Miss it and the year is gone — there's no late window.

  3. Receive the registration number

    Registration confirms your activities are lodged. It is not an approval — you remain responsible for eligibility.

  4. Claim in the tax return

    Your agent enters the registration number in the R&D Tax Incentive schedule of the company tax return, and the ATO processes the offset.

  5. Expect scrutiny

    Both bodies review claims before or after registration, and larger claims can be published in the ATO's R&D tax transparency reports.

Common founder mistakes with R&D claims

Most failed R&D tax incentive app development claims die from one of these five:

  • Claiming routine development. The whole build goes in because "software feels innovative". Reviewers disagree, and clawbacks follow.
  • Missing the registration deadline. The 10-month window is absolute — founders who discover the program late lose the year entirely.
  • No contemporaneous records. A claim without experiment notes is an opinion, not evidence.
  • Wrong structure. Trading as a sole trader means no claim at all; the R&D entity must be a company.
  • Treating registration as approval. The registration number isn't a tick of eligibility — audits come later, and sometimes years later.

Run the experiments, keep the notes, register on time, and let your tax agent pressure-test the claim before it goes in. That order works — and it's the same discipline that keeps any R&D tax incentive app development claim standing when the ATO comes knocking.
