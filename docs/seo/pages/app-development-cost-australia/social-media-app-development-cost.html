The short answer: social media app development cost

International studios typically quote social platforms from about $50,000 for a lean MVP up to $500,000 and beyond for a full-scale platform — an MVP being the smallest version that still works for real people. Convert that to an Australian build and the floor sits a little higher, but the shape holds. Here's what separates the cheap end from the expensive end:

$50k–$120kLean MVP — profiles, posts, basic feed
$120k–$250kMid-scale — messaging, media uploads, groups
$250k–$500k+Full platform — feeds ranked by algorithm, live video

Notice what drives the tiers apart: not the number of screens, but the systems behind them. A feed that simply shows the newest posts is cheap. A feed that decides what each person most wants to see is an engineering project of its own.

Niche community app

  • Chronological feed, groups, direct messages

  • Moderation starts small — often the founder's own eyeballs

Full public platform

  • Moderation must be staffed and automated from day one
  • Servers and safety costs scale with every sign-up

Profiles, feeds, moderation: what drives the price

Each core piece of a social app carries real engineering weight. International cost breakdowns put user profiles at roughly $5,000–$12,000, messaging systems at $15,000–$45,000, media upload and processing at $20,000–$70,000, and a news feed algorithm at $20,000–$60,000. Chat is a big chunk of that middle band — our guide to adding chat to an app explains why real-time messaging costs what it does. Add an admin dashboard (your back-office screen for managing reports and members) and you can see how the tiers form.

Two things founders underestimate. First, the "social graph" — the map of who follows whom — touches every feature, so adding it later costs more than planning it early. Second, real-time features like chat need infrastructure that stays awake and responsive; that's a server conversation as much as a code conversation, which we explain in whether apps need a server.

There's a quieter category too: the paid services underneath. Push notifications, media storage, spam filtering and analytics each charge monthly or by volume, and a social app leans on all of them. Studios budget these third-party subscriptions at hundreds to a couple of thousand dollars a month for a growing platform — small lines individually, a real bill together, and they start on day one.

A niche community version: the affordable build

Here's the version we most often recommend: profiles, a simple feed, one or two interest groups, direct messages between members, and reporting buttons. That's a genuine social app, it's buildable inside the MVP band, and — critically — it matches what a small community actually needs in year one.

A pattern we see monthly

A founder tells us their app will be "like Instagram, but for ___". Ten minutes later it turns out their 400-member community wants a place to post, comment and meet up. Those are different projects with a ten-fold price difference. The founders who last build the second one first.

Why moderation is a cost you can't skip

The moment strangers can post, someone will post something harmful. Moderation — reviewing, removing and escalating bad content — isn't an optional feature you add when things go well. It's the price of admission for hosting other people's conversations, and platforms that skip it end up paying in brand damage, store removal, or legal exposure.

Budget-wise, expect a reporting system and admin tools in the build, and human or automated review as an ongoing cost. If you want to see how seriously regulators treat this, the eSafety Commissioner's Safety by Design principles set out what user-safety-first platforms build in from the start. In Australia, that's not just good ethics — it's the direction the law is heading.

Your bills grow with your member count

A social app's running costs are a function of its size. Every member's photos, videos and messages live on servers you pay for monthly. More members means more storage, more bandwidth, more moderation queue time. The studios' numbers agree on this point: ongoing platform costs routinely rival the original build within a couple of years of healthy growth.

Then there's the store's cut. If your community ever pays for anything inside the app — a premium tier, an event ticket — Apple and Google take a percentage of each sale, and that percentage applies forever. It's not a development cost, but it belongs in any honest social-app budget, because it changes what each member is worth to you.

A social app is priced twice — once to build it, and forever to run it. Budget the second number before you commit to the first.

Start with a group, not a platform

Our standard advice: prove the community exists before you build the platform. Run the group where it costs you nothing. Watch what members actually ask for. Then build the smallest app that serves those asks, and let the running costs grow only as fast as the membership does. The social apps that die rarely die of missing features — they die of empty rooms and unpaid server bills.

A practical first step that costs nothing: spend ninety days running the community manually — a spreadsheet, a group chat, a weekly post. If you're still enjoying it at day ninety, and people are asking for a better home, that's your signal the app budget will be money spent on something real. If it fizzles, you've saved yourself six figures and learned something useful about your audience instead.
