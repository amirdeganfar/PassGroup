"Cross-platform" sounds like the budget option, and "native" sounds like the premium one. That's true about half the time. In native vs cross platform app development, the cheaper route up front is usually cross-platform, but it isn't always the cheaper route over three years. Here's how the two compare on build cost, running cost and performance, and how to pick without a computer science degree.

The short version: for most first apps, one cross-platform build is the sensible start. Native earns its extra cost when your app leans hard on the phone itself. Native vs cross platform app development, in plain English

A native app is built separately for each phone type, in the language that platform prefers: Swift for iPhone, Kotlin for Android. You end up with two apps and two codebases (the code is the app's instruction set). A cross-platform app is built once, using a tool like Flutter or React Native, and that single codebase runs on both iPhone and Android.

You may also hear "hybrid". That's an older style where a website is wrapped inside an app shell. It's cheap, but it often feels slow, so most studios now use true cross-platform tools instead. From here on, "cross-platform" means those modern tools.

Side by side: build cost, running cost, performance

Native (two apps)

  • Two codebases, two builds, two rounds of testing
  • Smoothest performance and first access to new phone features
  • Higher build cost and two things to maintain
  • Best for games, heavy animation, camera or sensor-driven apps

Cross-platform (one app)

  • One codebase, one team, shared testing
  • Close to native for most business apps
  • Lower build cost and one set of fixes
  • Best for bookings, shops, member apps, internal tools

Performance is where the old arguments live. A few years ago, cross-platform apps often felt laggy. Today, tools like Flutter draw their own screens and run fast enough that most users can't tell the difference in everyday apps. The gap shows up in demanding cases: long scrolling lists packed with images, smooth 3D, or constant sensor readings. If your app is forms, lists and payments, performance is rarely the deciding factor.

Why cross-platform often costs less up front

The saving comes from sharing work. Design, the server behind the app, project management and most of the screens get built once instead of twice. Industry guides often quote a saving of around a third compared with two native builds. Treat that as a rule of thumb, because it shrinks if your app needs lots of phone-specific features.

It's also faster to launch. One team building one app means one timeline, which matters if you're testing an idea. That's why MVP builds (the smallest working version of your app) are so often cross-platform: you learn from real users sooner and spend less finding out.

A pattern we see at consultations

A founder arrives convinced they need native "because it's proper". We ask which phone feature the app actually depends on. Most of the time the answer is a login, a list, a payment and a notification. All of that works well cross-platform, and the saved budget goes into design and testing instead.

When native is worth the extra money

Native pays off when the phone's hardware is the product. Think fast games, augmented reality, heavy video or audio work, or an app that must use a brand-new iPhone feature on launch day. Cross-platform tools can reach most of these features through add-ons, but add-ons lag behind new releases, and workarounds cost hours.

Native also makes sense if you already know you want two very different experiences, one made for each platform. And if you're starting with one platform only, going native on it is a fair choice. Our fee overview in what Apple and Google charge app owners covers the store side of that decision.

One more thing to weigh is people. Finding a good native iPhone developer and a good native Android developer means two hiring jobs, and if one leaves, half your app has no owner. A cross-platform app has one team and one body of code, which makes handovers simpler. Whoever builds it, make sure you own the code. That's covered in who owns your app's code.

How the choice changes your maintenance bill

This is the part most comparisons skip. Every phone update from Apple or Google can need a check, and sometimes a fix. With two native apps, you pay for that twice. With one cross-platform app, you pay once, though you also depend on the tool's makers (Google backs Flutter, Meta backs React Native) to keep up with phone updates.

The bigger picture is in our guide to yearly app maintenance costs. As a rough planning habit, expect upkeep to track the number of codebases you own. Fewer codebases usually means a smaller yearly bill, and a slower creep of costs as the app grows.

Native costs more to build and more to keep. Cross-platform costs less on both, until your app needs something the tool can't reach cleanly.

What most founder-budget apps actually choose

Most apps at the $5,000–$20,000 mark are built cross-platform, or built native on one platform first. That's driven by money, not taste. A lean budget can't fund two native apps without cutting features, and features are what users judge you on.

A simple way to decide:

  1. List the phone features you truly need

    Camera, GPS, push notifications, payments. Most are well supported by both approaches.

  2. Flag anything unusual

    Heavy animation, Bluetooth gadgets, AR. These are the cases where native may win.

  3. Ask your developer to justify the choice

    A good studio explains why in plain words and shows what you'd lose by choosing the other way. Our piece on what affects app development cost is a useful checklist before that chat.

For the official picture on one cross-platform tool, Flutter's own site explains what it does and which platforms it reaches. Whichever way you lean, get both options quoted so you can see the real difference for your app.
