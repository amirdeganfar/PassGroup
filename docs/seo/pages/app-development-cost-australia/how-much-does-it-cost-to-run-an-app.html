"The app's live — why are we still paying for it?" A client emailed me that three months after launch day. The honest answer: a finished app is never finished. It needs rent, care and renewals for as long as it exists. So, how much does it cost to run an app? Budget roughly $100–$500 a month for a typical small app — less while it's quiet, more as it gets busy — and this page turns that band into a first-year budget you can actually plan around.

The bill has four parts: hosting (the server your data lives on), maintenance (updates, fixes and yearly phone-system retests), store fees, and support — a human who answers when a user gets stuck. Where your app sits in the band depends mostly on how busy it is and how much it does. Hosting and infrastructure

Hosting is the app's rent. A server — a computer in a data centre that holds logins, content and orders — stays on even when the app is closed. In practice that rent buys a handful of cloud services: compute (the machinery that runs your app's logic), a database (where the records live), storage (photos and files), and the plumbing that sends notifications. The stores themselves host the app download for free — Apple notes it offers unlimited hosting and bandwidth even for free apps — so what you're paying for here is the machinery behind the app. For a young app that's typically tens of dollars a month, climbing with users and data. Let the hosting bill lapse and the app simply disappears — nothing else in your budget works like that. Not sure why an app needs a server at all? Here's the plain-English explanation.

Maintenance: updates, bugs and OS releases

Apple and Google each ship a major phone update every year, and every one of those asks your app to prove it still works. Real users also find bugs no tester caught. A common planning rule is 10–20% of the build cost per year, spread across fixes, updates and small improvements. A fix round might mean a crash report investigated on Monday and shipped by Friday; a retest year means walking your key screens on the new phone system and patching what creaks. Store fees and third-party subscriptions

The store accounts are small and predictable: Apple charges US$99 a year, Google a one-off US$25 sign-up. Commissions apply only when you sell digital goods through the stores, and for most small developers they run 15% of the sale. Around the hosting sit the third-party subscriptions — payments, maps, messages, email — each renewing on its own date, in its own dashboard. Get the full list written down at handover, with prices, so no invoice after launch is a stranger. The stores deserve one more line in your plan: if your app sells digital goods, price your margins at 85% of the sale price, because that's roughly what lands with you after commission.

Support: who answers the emails?

Support time is the line that surprises new founders most. Every app with users generates questions: lost logins, refund requests, "does it work on my phone?". The time costs nothing in code but plenty in focus, so decide early who owns the inbox — you, a team member, or the developer on a retainer. Write the decision down, because "we'll work it out later" becomes "nobody answered for a fortnight" surprisingly fast. Even an hour a week, budgeted honestly, beats an hour a week absorbed silently.

The first support inbox I ran for a client was me, on my phone, between meetings. It worked — but we should have budgeted for it from day one.

Planning a first-year budget, quarter by quarter

Sketch the year before launch and the running costs become routine. A simple pattern that works:

QuarterWhat happensBudget note
Q1 — launchStore accounts, hosting setup, launch-week fixesThe busy quarter — hold extra aside
Q2 — settleHosting, small fixes, first user feedbackUsually the cheapest quarter
Q3 — retestA phone OS update lands; retest and patchPlan a fix round regardless
Q4 — renewRenewals, subscription reviews, next-year planTrim what you don't use

Turn that sketch into numbers with the business.gov.au guide to creating a budget — the template works as well for an app as for a café. Your running-budget checklist:

  • Hosting for 12 months, at the growing rate, not the free-tier rate
  • Maintenance — a retainer or the 10–20% rule of thumb
  • Store account fees and any third-party subscriptions
  • Support time — whose hours, how many, at what cost
  • A buffer — roughly 10% for the surprise you can't name yet

And when someone asks you why an app still costs money after launch, you'll have the short answer ready: the build was a one-off, but keeping an app alive is rent and care — $100–$500 a month for a typical small app — and both are cheap compared to starting over.
