What pushes the price up: video, wearables and programs

Three things quietly turn a cheap app into an expensive one.

Video. The moment your app streams workouts, you're paying for more than a video player. Files need to live somewhere fast (a content delivery network — servers placed around the world so playback doesn't buffer), and every minute a member watches costs a little in bandwidth. Streaming is the single feature most often underestimated by first-time founders.

Wearables. Pulling heart-rate and step data from a watch means integrating with Apple Health, Google Health Connect, or a device maker's own kit — each a separate engineering job with its own quirks. Background syncing also has to be gentle on phone batteries, which takes skilled work.

Personalised programs. A fixed list of workouts is simple. Programs that adapt to what a user did last week mean rules, data pipelines and testing — expect that alone to add tens of thousands.

A simple program-and-tracker version: the budget build

Here's what the first row of that table looks like as a feature list: accounts and profiles, a library of programs a trainer can assign, workout logging, progress charts, and reminders. Nothing exotic — and genuinely useful. At Melbourne agency rates this is typically a three-to-four-month build with two developers and a designer.

This is also the version we steer most first-timers towards. A coach I worked with in Brunswick launched with exactly this set, sold it to her existing clients at $15 a month, and used that revenue to fund everything that came after. Her words at launch: "My clients wanted my program, not my ambition."

Know what the budget build deliberately leaves out: live classes, custom animations, meal tracking, and anything that syncs with a watch. That's not a compromise you're stuck with — it's a sequencing choice. Every one of those can bolt on later if the first version earns its keep. What matters at launch is that the core loop works: client opens app, sees today's session, does it, logs it, sees progress. Apps that nail that boring loop keep users. Apps with twenty features and a shaky core lose them.

Adding wearable sync: where the money goes

The mid-tier version — say $80,000–$150,000 — usually means video or wearables, occasionally both. Subscription payments add a slice too: recurring billing, access rules (who's allowed into what), and the admin screens to manage members. None of this is glamorous, and all of it is where fitness apps earn their keep, because subscriptions are how fitness apps make money.

A word on each adder, from experience. Video is usually the bigger half: encoding every workout so it plays fast on a phone in a garage gym, thumbnailing it, organising it into programs — the filming itself is on you, but the plumbing around it is real work. Wearables are the fiddlier half: two phones running the same watch can report different heart rates, and your app has to look sensible when they do. Budget a testing line for device quirks, not just for the integration itself.

The bills after launch: video hosting and subscriptions

The build fee buys the code once. After that, the app has running costs, and fitness apps carry heavier ones than most: video bandwidth that scales with how popular you get, server costs, store fees on every subscription, and an annual round of fixes when Apple and Google update their operating systems. Plan on 10–20% of the build cost per year — our app maintenance cost per month breakdown shows what that looks like as a monthly figure.

The pattern we see: founders budget hard for the build and treat everything after launch as a surprise. The running costs aren't a surprise — they're the second half of the same budget.

Worth knowing, too: money you spend keeping and improving an app is generally an ordinary business expense, and the ATO explains what you can claim as business deductions. Ask your accountant how it applies to you — we're developers, not tax agents. To work the ongoing spend into your numbers, Moneysmart's free budget planner is a decent place to start.

Cheaper first versions that still sell

If $30,000–$80,000 is out of reach right now, there are a few honest shortcuts. First, trim the feature list until it fits — a program library and logging alone can come in under the band. Second, some studios offer a white-label option: an existing fitness platform rebranded as yours, quoted from a few thousand dollars to set up plus a monthly fee. It launches fast and costs little, but you're renting the floor plan — limited custom features, and the platform provider sits between you and your roadmap. For testing an idea it can make sense; for building a business, most owners eventually outgrow it.

Third, get the sequence right before you spend anything. If you want a sense of the whole journey from idea to launch, read how much it costs to make an app in Australia, and for the deeper line-by-line detail, our app development cost breakdown pulls a real quote apart.

What not to do: don't buy a "fitness app for $5,000" deal that turns out to be a rebranded template you don't fully own. If the code isn't yours, your app isn't yours.
