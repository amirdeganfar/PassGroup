App development cost breakdown: the five lines on every bill

Almost every professional quote, from a freelancer or a 40-person agency, is built from the same five lines:

Line itemWhat it coversTypical share of budget
Discovery & designWorking out what to build, then designing every screen15–25%
The buildThe hours writing the actual code50–60%
Testing & launchMaking sure it works, then getting it in the stores10–15%
Project managementSomeone keeping scope, schedule and budget honest5–10%
After-launch careUpdates, fixes and hosting once real users arriveOngoing

Percentages vary — a design-heavy consumer app leans harder on the first line, an integration-heavy business tool leans on the build. What shouldn't vary is presence. A quote missing a line isn't cheaper. The cost is still in there; it's just wearing a disguise.

The last row deserves a flag of its own. After-launch care isn't a single payment — it's the ongoing budget from the day you go live: yearly system updates from Apple and Google, bug fixes as real users find real problems, and hosting for the servers behind your app. As a planning figure, expect 10–20% of the build cost per year. Any quote that pretends this line doesn't exist is deferring a cost, not removing one.

Discovery and design: where the budget starts

Discovery is the industry word for "working out what to build before building it" — workshops, a feature list, and a map of how users will move through the app. Some studios run it as a formal phase costing $5,000–$15,000; leaner teams fold it into the first sprint. Either way it's the cheapest mistake insurance you'll ever buy, because changing a plan costs hours and changing a finished app costs weeks.

Design follows: wireframes (simple sketches of each screen), then the finished visual design. Clean design built from standard components typically runs $5,000–$15,000. Fully custom design — your brand's look, animations, unusual layouts — starts around $15,000 and climbs with every screen. A first version rarely needs the custom end.

The build itself: features, hours and rates

This is the engine of the quote, and it's governed by one formula: hours × hourly rate. Australian agencies typically charge $120–$200 per hour. Each feature is estimated in hours — a login system might take 30, an in-app chat over 100 — and the hours become dollars.

To make that concrete, here are typical Australian agency figures to build single features, at the middle of their usual ranges:

$5kLogin & user accounts
$8kCard payments (Stripe)
$11kMaps & GPS tracking
$15kIn-app chat

Two things the numbers should tell you. First, features aren't equal — chat costs three times a login. Second, nothing above is exotic; these are ordinary features, and complex ones (live video, marketplaces, AI) run far higher. This is also why "just one more small feature" is the most expensive sentence in software: every addition touches design, testing and the backend (the servers and databases working behind the scenes).

Ask how the build is priced, too. A fixed price locks the number but also locks the scope — change your mind later and you're paying change fees. Time and materials billing (paying for hours actually worked) flexes as your thinking sharpens, but needs an agreed cap. Neither is wrong; what matters is that you know which one you signed.

Testing, launch and store setup

Testing means checking the app on real devices — many makes, many screen sizes, many system versions — and fixing what breaks. Professional teams budget 10–15% of the build for it. Cutting this line is how apps end up with one-star reviews that mention the word "crash".

Launch adds fixed, knowable costs: the Apple developer account (about AU$149 per year) and Google's one-off fee (roughly US$25, around AU$40), plus the hours to prepare store listings and pass each store's review. If the app's name matters to your plans, registering it as a trade mark through IP Australia's trade mark guide starts at $250 and takes at least seven months — worth starting early.

What a quote should show (and what it shouldn't)

A healthy quote shows

  • Each of the five lines priced separately
  • Hours and hourly rate for the build
  • Exactly which features are included — and which aren't
  • What happens after launch: fixes, support, handover
  • A clear statement that you own the code and design

Worth a hard question

  • One total with no breakdown
  • A rate with no hours attached
  • Testing or project management "included free" (where did the hours go?)
  • Silence about who owns the intellectual property
  • Payment in full before anything is delivered

Compare two quotes by matching line to line, not total to total. The cheaper quote often has fewer hours because it quietly excludes testing or a feature you assumed was in.

A worked example: one feature, one line, one price

Let's price one ordinary feature the way a professional quote would: taking card payments inside the app.

Design first: two or three screens — the payment form, a confirmation, an error state — about $1,200 at standard rates. The build: roughly 55 hours to connect Stripe (a widely used payment service), handle failed cards, and record every sale in your admin screen; at a typical $150 per hour that's about $8,250. Testing: payments fail in creative ways, so it earns a careful 10 hours, roughly $1,400. Add a slice of project management and the line on the quote reads approximately $11,000 — and now you can ask any studio to defend it, line by line.

That's the whole trick of reading quotes: every line is hours × rate, wearing different clothes. When you can see the hours, you can negotiate the scope. When you can't, you're negotiating blind.

One warning about this example: it assumes the app already exists. The same payment feature inside a marketplace — where money splits between buyers and sellers — involves financial rules that can double or triple the hours. Context changes everything, which is why honest estimates start with scope, not a price list.
