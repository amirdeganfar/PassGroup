Why games price differently from every other app

A useful app succeeds when it works. A game succeeds when it's fun, and fun can't be specified on a feature list — it's found by building, playing, adjusting, and building again. That loop means more iterations, and every iteration costs hours.

The team shape is different too. An ordinary app might need two developers and a designer. A game needs artists, an animator, a sound designer and a gameplay programmer whose entire job is making the controls feel right. Different specialists, more of them, longer timelines — mid-weight games typically take 12–24 months.

The tools soften the bill a little. Game engines — Unity, Godot, Unreal — are the pre-built factories of game development, and each is free until your game earns real money, at which point a revenue share kicks in. So the engine itself is rarely the cost driver; the hours of skilled people feeding it art, levels and tuning are.

Simple puzzle games vs mid-weight 3D games

The puzzle path is the honest way in: one core mechanic, a handful of menus, maybe fifty hand-made levels. Small teams ship these in three to six months. It's also the category where the store's economics still work for newcomers — casual players download in volume.

Mid-weight 3D is a different animal. Every character model, environment and animation is hand-built; industry rate cards put a single polished 3D character anywhere from a few thousand dollars to ten times that, and custom soundtracks from around $1,000 to $20,000. Multiply by everything a game contains and the band explains itself.

The budget-killer in games is scope, not rate. "One more level type" can cost more than the entire first version. Decide what's in the game before anyone opens an editor.

Art, sound and levels: where the money actually goes

Ask a studio where a game budget vanishes and you'll hear the same list: art first, then level content, then sound, then the endless tuning that makes the first three feel good together. Code is rarely the biggest line.

Spends that pay off

  • One polished core mechanic, tested with real players
  • Consistent art style (even a cheap style, done well)
  • Sound — the cheapest "feel" multiplier there is
  • A small first release you can update

Spends that sink first games

  • Multiplayer in version one
  • Photorealistic anything
  • Hundreds of levels before anyone has played ten
  • Licensed music and voice acting before launch

A live game never stops costing money

Here's the part most first-timers miss: for a modern mobile game, launch is the start of the spend, not the end. Players expect new levels, events and fixes for as long as they play. Studios call this live ops, and for successful games it can cost as much per year as the original build. Your store account, servers for anything online, and the yearly iOS and Android updates all continue too — the same running costs behind every app's ongoing cost lines.

How games earn also shapes the ongoing picture. Free-to-download games funded by ads and in-app purchases need ad networks wired in and tuned — and the store takes its cut of every purchase. Paid-upfront games dodge the ad plumbing but carry all the marketing weight themselves. Decide which model you're running before the build starts, because it changes what gets engineered.

One ownership note worth an early check: your game's code and art are protected automatically by copyright, and the Australian Government's IP body explains how intellectual property works. Make sure your contract says you own what you paid for.

Test the idea before you build the game

The cheapest game you'll ever "build" is a paper prototype: a playable rough cut of the core loop, built in days, that tells you whether the mechanic is fun at all. Studios do this formally — a vertical slice, one polished level standing in for the whole game — and investors expect it. The logic is brutal arithmetic: if the one-mechanic version isn't fun, two hundred more levels won't fix it.

You can run a cheaper version of this test yourself. Describe the core loop to ten strangers — "you flick the frog, it lands on lily pads, pads sink" — and watch whether they ask to play it. Enthusiasm before playtesting is cheap; enthusiasm after launch is called marketing spend.
