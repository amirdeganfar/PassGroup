A build quote is not a budget. The hidden costs of app development — store accounts, servers, yearly updates, licences — sit outside the quote, and they arrive on schedule whether you have planned for them or not. Each one is small on its own. Together, they can add a meaningful slice to your first year, so it pays to see them coming.

The quote you receive covers the work: design, build, testing. What it cannot cover is everything the finished app needs to stay alive, because those costs belong to different companies on different calendars. We walk every Pass Group client through the full first-year picture before a line of code is written — it is part of our free consultation — because a founder who knows the running costs makes sharper build decisions. This page is that walkthrough. For the build side of the money, start with what an app really costs in Australia.

The short version

  • Your quote buys the build; a separate budget keeps it alive.
  • Store accounts charge just to publish: Apple yearly, Google once-off.
  • Hosting and maintenance never stop — budget roughly 10–20% of the build per year.
  • Every major phone update asks your app to prove it still works.

Why the quote isn't the final number

Developers quote what they can control: the hours of design, build and testing in front of them. Three of the biggest after-costs sit outside that scope. The app stores charge their own fees to distribute your app. The cloud charges monthly to keep it online. And time — next year's updates and fixes — cannot be priced until the app exists. None of this is a trick. It is simply two different bills: one upfront, one ongoing. The full app cost breakdown shows where build dollars go, so this page stays on the ones the quote leaves out.

Store fees and developer accounts

Both stores charge you for the right to publish. Apple runs its developer program on a US$99 per year membership; Google charges a one-off sign-up fee, currently US$25. For a lean app these are rounding errors — they only sting when nobody planned for them at all, or when the renewal lapses by accident and the app wobbles mid-year. One detail matters more than the money: keep the accounts in your name, under your business. If the accounts sit with a developer and the relationship ends, moving them later is possible but rarely quick, and while the paperwork drags your app sits frozen in the store. The Apple Developer Program and Google Play's fee schedule spell out the current rates in full.

Hosting, servers and third-party subscriptions

Almost every app rents space online — a server that holds logins, content and orders even when the app itself is closed. Hosting for a young app is typically modest, in the tens of dollars a month, and it grows with users and data. Wondering why an app needs a server at all? Here is the plain-English answer. Around the hosting sit the third-party subscriptions: payment processing, maps, message services, email tools. Most offer free tiers while you are small, then start charging as your users arrive — and each one renews on its own date, in its own dashboard, with its own invoice. Ask your developer to list every subscription in the handover document, with prices. You should be able to name each line before the bills become yours.

Updates, bug fixes and phone OS changes

Apple and Google each ship a major phone update every year, and every update quietly asks your app to prove it still works. Real users also find bugs no tester ever could. That ongoing care is maintenance, and it is the biggest of the hidden costs. A common planning rule is 10–20% of the build cost per year, spread across hosting, fixes and small improvements. A quiet information app can sit near the bottom of that range; a busy booking or marketplace app with payments will sit near the top. From the studio

A retail founder I worked with budgeted to the quote — to the dollar — and launched beautifully. Then came the first iPhone update, a payment add-on nobody had scoped, and a photo library that tripled the hosting bill. Her app was fine; her spreadsheet was not. Content, assets and licences you supply

Some costs live on your side of the fence, and they are easy to forget because they feel like things rather than bills. The words on your screens. Product photos. A logo update so the app matches the brand. Licences for fonts, stock images or specialised software — most cost little, but they are recurring, and a lapsed renewal can break something visual overnight. Put every one of them on the same calendar as the store renewals, and hand the list to whoever manages the books. One more line founders forget: training and questions in the first month. Someone has to learn the admin side, and that someone is on your payroll.

Budgeting for the hidden costs of app development

The fix is not a bigger quote — it is a second budget line, written on day one. List every recurring cost, give each a yearly figure, and add them up separately from the build. The start-up costs guide at business.gov.au is a sensible template for exactly this habit: it walks a new business through naming every cost, one-off and ongoing, before the money is committed. As a starting table for an app:

Hidden costWhat it pays forPlanning figure for year one
Store accountsPublishing rights on Apple and GoogleUS$99/yr + US$25 once — small, but always there
HostingThe always-on server behind the appTens of dollars a month while young; grows with users
MaintenanceUpdates, fixes, OS retestsRoughly 10–20% of build cost per year
Licences and assetsFonts, stock media, specialist toolsBudget a few hundred dollars a year
Your time and contentWords, photos, decisions, sign-offsReal hours — write them into the plan

Two habits keep the table honest: review it each quarter, and move any line that's growing faster than the app is. Budgeting well is mostly noticing early.

Do

  • Keep the store accounts in your own business name
  • Ask for a first-year running-cost estimate with every quote
  • Set maintenance aside as a fixed monthly figure

Don't

  • Treat the quote as the last invoice you will ever see
  • Carry subscriptions you cannot name or cancel
  • Skip a year of OS testing to save money — it costs more later

None of these costs are reasons to avoid building. They are reasons to build with eyes open, because founders who plan for them ship calmer — nothing after launch turns out to be a surprise.
