Most app disputes don't start with bad code. They start with a sentence in a contract that nobody read twice. An app development contract is the signed agreement that says what gets built, what you pay, who owns the result and how either side can walk away. This page covers the six parts to check before you sign. If you're still working out what the whole build should cost, start with our complete guide to app development costs in Australia.

One plain note: this is general information, not legal advice. For a contract worth tens of thousands of dollars, pay a lawyer to read it. At Pass Group, a Melbourne app studio, we're happy to talk through any contract on a free consultation, but we're not your lawyer.

The short version

  • Check six things: scope, change requests, payments, ownership, warranty and exit terms.
  • Tie every payment to something you can see working.
  • Get ownership of the code and design in writing. Don't assume it.
  • If a clause confuses you, ask now. Once you sign, it's binding.

The five clauses every app development contract needs

Contracts run to many pages, but five clauses carry most of the risk. A sixth, the exit terms, matters only when things go wrong, and that's exactly when you'll want it. Here's the whole set at a glance:

ClauseThe question it answersWhat good looks like
ScopeWhat exactly am I buying?A feature list and a list of exclusions
Change requestsWhat happens when I want something new?A written process with a price before work starts
PaymentsWhen do I pay, and for what?Each payment tied to a delivered milestone
IP and source codeWho owns the app?You own it once you've paid
Warranty and supportWho fixes bugs after launch?A set bug-fix period, in writing
Exit termsCan I leave if it goes wrong?A fair way out, with your work handed over

Business.gov.au makes a point worth repeating: a clear contract makes for a good business relationship because both sides know what they've agreed to. Its guide to negotiating a contract also warns against signing something you're unsure about in the hope of sorting it out later. Terms are legally binding once signed. We'll take each clause in turn.

Scope and change requests: where budgets blow out

Scope is the list of what the developer will build. If a feature isn't on the list, assume it isn't included. Good contracts attach the quote or the app development proposal and say so in the text. Vague wording like "a full-featured booking app" is a problem. It can mean ten screens or forty.

Then comes the part founders skip: change requests. You will want to change something. Everyone does. The contract should say how a change gets requested, priced and approved before any work starts. Without that, you get surprise invoices. The line-by-line guide to reading an app quote shows where exclusions hide, and it's worth reading beside your contract.

Payment terms and what triggers each payment

Payments should follow progress, not the calendar. A common pattern for a mid-sized build is a deposit of around 20–40% to start, progress payments as phases finish, and a final slice at handover. These are typical ranges, not rules. The point is that each payment is triggered by something you can check.

  1. Deposit on signing

    Books the team and covers discovery and design.

  2. Payment on approved designs

    Triggered when you've signed off the screens, not when the developer says they're done.

  3. Payment on a working build

    Triggered when you can open the app on your own phone and try it.

  4. Final payment at handover

    Triggered when you hold the code, the accounts and the launch files.

Check three more details. How soon is an invoice due? Is there a late fee? And does the contract allow the developer to pause work if you pay late? None of these is sinister, but all should be in writing.

IP and source code: the ownership clause

This is the clause people assume is covered, and often isn't. IP Australia explains that in Australia, IP created by a contractor belongs to the contractor unless the contract says otherwise. That means if you pay a developer and the contract is silent, they may still own the code they wrote for you.

Look for one sentence: that on full payment, ownership of the code, design and related material passes to you. Also check who owns pre-existing tools the developer reuses, since those usually stay with them.

Our guide on who owns the app code covers this in more depth, including developer accounts and handover. Make sure the app store accounts are registered in your name, not the developer's.

Warranty, bug fixes and post-launch support

Every new app has bugs, so ask what happens when they show up. Many studios include a short bug-fix window after launch, often 30 to 90 days, but it varies, so check yours. Make sure the contract says what counts as a bug (something that doesn't work as agreed) versus a new feature (something you'd like added). The first is covered; the second is a change request.

Ongoing support is a separate matter. Updates for new phone software, security patches and hosting usually cost extra. If the contract is silent, ask what the monthly figure would be. Our page on the hidden costs of app development lists what to expect after launch.

From our side of the desk

A founder once asked us to add "just a small chat feature" three weeks before launch. Because the contract had a change process, we priced it that afternoon, she approved it in writing, and nobody was surprised later. The same request without that clause is how friendly projects turn tense.

Exit terms: what happens if it goes wrong

Nobody signs planning to leave, but projects stall, people get sick and priorities change. Exit terms decide how painful that is. Look for these:

  • You can end the contract with written notice, and you pay only for work done to date.
  • On exit, you receive all work completed so far, including code and design files.
  • The developer can't hold your work back over a disputed invoice for another part of the job.
  • There's a clear way to settle disputes, such as talking first, then mediation, before court.

The details of what's fair can vary, which is another reason to get a lawyer to review the final version. If a developer has already vanished on you, our page on what to do when an app developer disappears shows how the right clauses make that easier to survive.

Before signing, read the contract once for the money, once for ownership and once for the way out. If any answer is "it doesn't say", ask for it to be added. A good developer will say yes without fuss.
