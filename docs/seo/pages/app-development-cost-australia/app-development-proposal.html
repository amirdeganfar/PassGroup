Proposal vs quote vs contract: the differences

Three documents, three jobs. Founders who mix them up tend to sign the wrong one at the wrong time:

DocumentWhat it isWhat it settles
ProposalThe studio's plan for your appScope, approach, timeline, price, ownership — the what and the how
QuoteA price for a defined slice of workThe number only — often with no scope or timeline attached
ContractThe legally binding agreementWhat each party must deliver, pay and own — enforceable in law

The order matters. A proposal convinces you the studio understands the problem; a quote puts a number on it; the contract makes the promises enforceable. And a written contract is worth the paper: in Australia a commercial contract binds you whether it's a handshake or on paper — business.gov.au says it plainly — which is exactly why the written one should carry the detail.

The eight parts every app development proposal needs

When the next proposal lands, tick these off. Solid ones contain:

  1. The problem and the outcome — your app's job in one paragraph, in your words
  2. Scope — features listed as must-have or later, platform choices (iPhone, Android or both) named
  3. Approach — how they'll work: discovery, design, builds in stages
  4. Timeline — phases with dates, and what "done" means at each step
  5. Price and payment schedule — the total, split by milestone rather than one lump
  6. The team — names and roles, and who stays on the project
  7. Ownership terms — code, design and accounts ending up yours, stated in writing
  8. Support terms — warranty period, bug fixing, and what maintenance costs after launch

Eight of eight is a good sign. Two or three missing is a conversation.

Scope, milestones and price on paper

The scope section is where a proposal earns its keep. It should say what's in, what's deliberately out, and what happens when you change your mind — because you will. Milestones then split the work into payable stages: a deposit to start, instalments tied to delivered work you can see and touch (a design you can click, a build you can install), and a final payment at handover. Our explainer on paying for an app in instalments shows how founders structure this safely. Then check the price matches the scope line for line — a discount that quietly drops testing or a second platform isn't a discount.

Who owns what: IP and accounts in writing

Intellectual property — IP for short — means who legally owns the app's code, designs and brand assets. The short version: it's yours only if the paperwork says so. A complete proposal states that code, design files and store accounts end up in your name, and it names the tools and third-party services the app depends on. If ownership language is missing, get it added before you sign, not after the final invoice — it's the one clause you can't retrofit cheaply.

What's missing when a proposal feels thin

Thin proposals share the same tells. The scope says "app like Uber" with no feature list. The timeline is one number ("12 weeks") with no phases. The price has no payment schedule. Ownership is never mentioned. Support reads as "we'll help you out" — an uncosted promise. And if a developer ever disappears mid-project, it's the written scope and ownership terms that decide whether you're left holding something you can hand to someone else.

Never accept "final price subject to discovery" as a whole proposal. Discovery is a legitimate first phase; an open-ended total is not. Ask what the ceiling of the price range assumes.

We've been handed one-page proposals to "beat" — a logo, a paragraph and a number. Every one was missing the same three things: a scope boundary, a milestone plan and an ownership clause. That's not a coincidence. Those are the three lines that cost the studio money to honour.

Turning the proposal into a contract

When a proposal survives your checks, the contract should repeat its promises in enforceable language:

  1. Fix the scope

    Attach the proposal's feature list as a schedule, so "in" and "out" are contractual.

  2. Tie payments to milestones

    Each instalment lands on demonstrated work you've seen running — never on calendar promises.

  3. Put ownership in writing

    IP assigned to you on final payment, store and hosting accounts in your name, source code released at handover.

  4. Get legal eyes on it

    A lawyer reads contracts every week; a few hundred dollars there is cheap next to a dispute.
