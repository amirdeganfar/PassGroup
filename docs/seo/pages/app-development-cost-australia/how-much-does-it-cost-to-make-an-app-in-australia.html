In February, a founder came to us with $15,000 in the bank and a spreadsheet with 42 features in it. Somewhere around row 12 she'd written the question that brought her here, the same one you're asking: how much does it cost to make an app in Australia? The honest answer isn't a number — it's a range with a decision attached. A testable first version starts around $5,000. A serious, polished product passes $150,000. For most first-time founders, the useful answer lives between $8,000 and $60,000. This page works out where you land in that range.

A warning before the numbers: most cost guides online quote $30,000 to $250,000, because that's what full-service agencies charge for complete builds. Those figures are honest, but they describe one kind of app. If you're funding this yourself, you deserve the whole picture — including the low end. So, how much does it cost to make an app in Australia?

Here are the four levels, from smallest to largest. These are typical figures for Australian teams, not fixed quotes.

What you're paying for when you pay for an app

Every app quote, at every budget, is the same four ingredients multiplied by an hourly rate. Australian developers typically charge $120–$200 per hour, so the hours matter more than anything else.

The ingredients: design (what the screens look like and how people move through them), development (the hours actually writing the code), testing (checking the app works on real phones, not just the developer's), and project management (someone keeping the whole thing on schedule). A simple first version might need 50–100 hours in total. A serious product needs 300–800 or more. Multiply out by the rate and the price bands explain themselves.

Testing deserves a defence, because it's the line most often cut. Phones come in hundreds of sizes and run dozens of system versions; an app that works on the developer's phone can still fail on yours. Good teams budget real hours for it. When a quote seems magically cheap, this is usually where the hours went missing — which is why we say: if you must trim, trim features, never testing.

Three real founder budgets: $8k, $18k and $60k

The $8,000 budget buys proof, not polish. One platform — iPhone or Android, whichever your audience prefers. Three or four screens. One core job done properly: a booking flow, a calculator, a paid membership sign-up. Design is clean and template-based. It's the version you hand to ten real customers to learn whether the idea holds.

The $18,000 budget is the sweet spot for most founders, and where that February spreadsheet eventually landed. You get both platforms from one shared codebase, user accounts, a basic admin screen so you can manage things yourself, and push notifications. It's the smallest app that feels like a real product — good enough to charge for, cheap enough to change.

The $60,000 budget gets you into serious-product territory: payments moving between different kinds of users, dashboards that report on what's happening, custom design that carries your brand, and integrations with other software you already use. This is usually a founder with early revenue or backers, not a first idea.

Founders don't run out of money on day one. They run out on the feature they didn't plan.

What happened to the spreadsheet

The founder with 42 features launched with five. We cut everything that didn't serve the app's single core job, built it for $14,000 in nine weeks, and she charged from day one. Her first hundred users ignored the two features she'd fought hardest to keep — and ignored them for something she'd planned to add "later". That insight shaped version two, and version two was worth building because version one had already earned money.

What changes the price fastest

Four things blow up a budget faster than anything else. Watch for them in your own feature list:

  • A second user type. The moment two different kinds of people use the same app — customer and provider, buyer and seller — you're building two apps' worth of screens, permissions and testing.
  • Real-time anything. Live chat, live tracking, live video. "Live" means servers working non-stop and far more testing.
  • Custom integrations. Connecting to your accounting software, a shipping company, a government system. Each connection is its own small project.
  • AI features. Smart recommendations and chatbots are genuinely useful, and genuinely expensive — often $15,000 or more on their own.

The first-year costs that surprise people

The build is the headline cost, not the whole cost. Within a year of launch you'll typically pay: the Apple developer fee (about AU$149 per year) and Google's one-off charge (roughly US$25, about AU$40); hosting, from $50 to $500 a month as your user base grows; and maintenance — Apple and Google update their systems yearly, and each update can break parts of your app, so budget another 10–20% of the build cost annually.

Two smaller ones worth knowing early. If your app's name matters to you, a trade mark from IP Australia starts at $250 and takes at least seven months to register — worth starting before launch, not after. And the tools inside your app (maps, SMS, payments) charge small usage fees that grow with your success. Cheap problems, but nicer to expect them.

A simple way to set your own budget

  1. Write down the one job your app does for its user. If you can't name it in a sentence, the scope isn't ready to price.
  2. Price the smallest honest version of that job using the table above — for most founders that's the $8,000–$20,000 band.
  3. Add the buffer: 15% for things you'll discover along the way, plus the first-year costs from the section above. That total is your real budget.
Real budget = (smallest honest version) + 15% buffer + first-year costs. Anything left over goes to marketing, not more features.
