The typical app development payment schedule

Most Australian studios follow the same three-part pattern: a deposit up front, a run of payments as stages are finished, and a final payment at launch. The exact percentages vary. As a rough guide, budget for a deposit somewhere around a fifth to a third of the total, but treat that as a range to discuss, not a rule.

  1. Deposit

    Paid on signing. It books your place in the studio's calendar and covers planning.

  2. Design milestone

    Paid when you approve the screens and the clickable prototype.

  3. Build milestones

    Paid as working features are delivered, often in two or three parts.

  4. Launch payment

    Paid when the app is tested and ready for the app stores.

  5. Final payment

    Paid on handover of the code, accounts and files.

A focused first build typically takes around 6-12 weeks, so you might see a payment every two to three weeks. That steady pace is a good sign. It means the studio is delivering in chunks you can check.

Deposits: why they exist and what's fair

A deposit isn't a trap. A studio turns away other work to hold your slot, and it spends real hours on planning before you've approved anything. A reasonable deposit covers that.

The problem is size. A deposit that covers the planning stage is fair. A deposit of half the project, before design exists, moves nearly all the risk to you. If the studio vanishes, you've paid for little. Our page on what to do if your app developer disappears shows how that goes wrong, and why smaller early payments are your best protection.

If a deposit is large, ask what it buys. A good answer names specific deliverables, like the scope document and the design phase, not just "to get started."

Milestone payments: paying for delivered work

A milestone is a clear checkpoint with a clear test: "the login and profile screens work on a real phone." You pay when the test passes. This keeps both sides honest. The studio gets paid for finished work, and you never pay far ahead of progress.

Good milestones are specific and checkable. "Design phase 50% complete" isn't one, because nobody can prove it. "Prototype approved by client" is. Write the test for each milestone into the contract so there's no debate when the invoice arrives.

If cash is tight, you can ask for smaller, more frequent milestones. Our guide to paying for an app in installments covers how to spread payments without hurting the build.

Final payment and handover: hold the line

The last payment is your leverage, so keep it tied to handover. That means the finished app, the source code (the files the app is built from), your store accounts, and any passwords and design files. Don't release it for a promise that these will "follow."

Check that ownership is clear in writing too. We explain who owns what in our page on who owns the app code. It's far easier to settle before the final payment than after.

A pattern we see

The smoothest projects we run have one thing in common: the client knew, before day one, exactly which invoice came with which milestone. Nobody was ever surprised by a bill, so nobody dreaded the next one.

Change requests: how extra features get billed

Almost every project gets a change request. You see the prototype and think of something better. That's healthy. What matters is how it's priced.

A fair process works like this: you describe the change, the studio estimates the time and cost in writing, and nothing starts until you say yes. The change then gets its own line in the schedule. If a studio just does the work and invoices later, you lose control of the budget. If you expect lots of changes, an hourly arrangement for that part may suit you better, as we explain in our guide to reading an app quote.

Payment terms worth negotiating

Everything in a payment schedule can be discussed. The government's own advice is to agree clear terms and write them down, so both sides understand their rights and duties. These are the terms we'd look at first:

  • Payment tied to a named deliverable, not a date
  • A clear number of days to pay each invoice
  • Written pricing for any change request before work starts
  • Final payment held until handover is complete
  • A fair process if a milestone is disputed
  • Large deposits before any design exists

Most good studios will happily agree to these. If one refuses all of them, treat that as useful information. A clear schedule protects the studio just as much as you, because it stops arguments before they start.
