If your budget is one pile of money and the choice is one product, here's the honest answer: a website nearly always costs less than an app. The app vs website cost gap shows up twice — in the build price and in the yearly bills — and for most small businesses the website wins both. Not always, though. This page lays out both sides so you can place your own idea on the right one, with numbers from our app development cost guide for Australia as the anchor.

The app vs website cost, in one short answer

A useful small-business website in Australia typically costs somewhere between $3,000 and $15,000 to build professionally. A useful app starts around $5,000–$20,000 for a lean first version and commonly lands at $20,000–$50,000 for a professional build. Then the running bills: a small website costs tens of dollars a month to keep online, while an app adds store accounts, heavier hosting and yearly maintenance on top. A third option — a web app, software that runs in the browser — sits between the two on price; we've priced what a web app costs in Australia on its own page.

So the default advice we give founders is simple: unless your idea genuinely needs phone features — camera, GPS, offline use, push notifications — start with the website. If you'd like a second opinion on which side your idea sits, our free consultation exists exactly for that question.

What a website costs vs what an app costs

The build prices differ for one big reason: an app is more moving parts. It has to be installed, it must live in a store with review rules, and it usually needs a backend — the hidden engine that stores accounts and data. A website is simpler to build, simpler to update and simpler to test. Typical Australian figures:

Website (build)

  • DIY template site: $0–$2,000 a year
  • Professional small-business site: $3,000–$15,000
  • Advanced site or online store: $20,000+

App (build)

  • Lean founder's first version: $5,000–$20,000
  • Simple professional app: $20,000–$50,000
  • Complex platform: $120,000+

One more build-side difference: a website works on every device with one build. An app for both iPhone and Android is two stores, two review cycles and (often) two codebases, unless you build cross-platform — one shared codebase that runs on both, which most studios now recommend.

The build is the headline; the running cost is where the gap widens. Here's a side-by-side of the yearly lines you'd actually pay:

The maintenance line is the one that surprises people. Phones get new operating systems every year and each update can break things, so an app needs developer attention on a schedule whether or not you change anything. There's friction on every change, too: an app update has to pass the store's review before it reaches your users, which can take a day or more. A website update goes live the moment you press save. When the website is the right first build

Most service businesses should hear this first: a good website already does more than they think. Bookings, payments, member logins, live menus and price lists — modern websites handle all of it in the browser. If your plan is "customers can find me, contact me and pay me", the website does that job at a fraction of the cost. The business.gov.au guide to ecommerce covers how online selling works for small business, store or no store.

Being found is the other quiet advantage. A website shows up in Google searches from the day it indexes — where people already look for a plumber, a physio or a café. An app is invisible to Google in the same way; it lives in a store's search, which is a smaller pond with fewer fishers. If new customers discovering you is the main goal, that difference alone decides the question.

The pattern we see win: website first, learn what customers actually use, then decide if an app earns its keep. It's the cheapest honest test of demand you can run.

When you genuinely need the app

Apps earn their keep when they use the phone itself. Run through this list honestly — three or more ticks and you're probably app territory:

  • People use it on the move, often with shaky internet
  • You need the camera, GPS or bluetooth
  • You want push notifications — messages that reach the phone even when the app is closed
  • Speed and a smooth feel are part of the product
  • Users open it daily or weekly, not once in a while

A café loyalty app ticks four of those. A plumber's brochure does none of them. Most local-business ideas sit somewhere between — and the honest placement is usually closer to the website end than founders expect.

Be careful with the middle answer too: "an app feels more professional". Customers don't judge you by which icon they tap — they judge whether the thing worked fast and did the job. A slow app with your logo still loses to a quick website with someone else's.

The both-at-once path most small businesses take

Here's the path we most often build: a proper mobile-friendly website now, and an app later once the website proves people want more. The website earns its keep immediately — it shows in Google searches from day one, something an app in a store can't do. It also collects exactly the evidence you'll want before paying for an app: which pages people use, where they get stuck, what they ask for. And if your idea turns out to need app-style behaviour in the browser, there's a middle ground too: a web app, which is a program that runs in the browser but behaves like software, with logins and dashboards of its own.

From experience

A fitness-studio owner came to us certain she needed an app — her members kept missing bookings. We built the website with a booking system instead, at about a third of the app quote. Six months later the missed-bookings complaints had gone quiet. Her "app" turned out to be a website problem the whole time, and she still has the budget she didn't spend.

Whichever side you land on, price both before you commit. Ten minutes of comparing — like you just did here — is the cheapest part of either project.
