App maintenance cost per year: the rule of thumb

The 10–20% figure floats around every studio and guide because it matches how the work lands: the newer and quieter the app, the closer to the floor; the busier and richer the app, the closer to — and sometimes past — the ceiling. Treat it as a planning band, not a quote. Two apps built for the same price can sit $5,000 apart within a single year, for reasons covered further down.

It also scales sensibly, which is what makes it useful. A bigger build has more screens to re-test, more code to re-fit, and more users to support, so its upkeep grows with it. You don't need to trust the percentage — you need to know it exists, and to ask any developer you hire which end of the band your app sits at, and why.

under ~$1,500a lean year — quiet app, few users, no new features
$1,500–$6,000a typical small app, billed steadily across the year
$6,000–$20,000+busy or complex apps — users, data, support, features

Those bands match what a small app shows on a monthly rhythm, too — our monthly maintenance breakdown splits the same numbers into twelve invoices if that's how you prefer to see it.

What maintenance actually covers each year

Maintenance sounds vague until you itemise it. Across a normal year, the bill pays for five things:

  • Keeping it compatible. New iPhone and Android software arrives every year, and it can shift how screens display and which permissions apps need. Your app gets re-tested against each release and re-fitted where it breaks.
  • Fixing what users find. Real people tap in ways no tester predicted. Bugs they surface get patched in small batches through the year.
  • Server and service costs. The app's data lives somewhere, and the services behind it (logins, notifications, analytics) charge monthly fees.
  • Store fees and renewals. Apple's developer account renews yearly and Google's listing needs maintaining; guidelines change and the app has to follow.
  • Small improvements. Little fixes to screens and flows that keep users from churning.

Notice what's not on that list: brand-new features, a redesign, or a security audit. Those are usually quoted separately (our guide to what an app redesign costs shows how wide those bands run), which is exactly where yearly budgets get blown — more on that in a moment.

A lean year vs a busy year: two example budgets

Nothing explains the spread better than the same app having two different years. Take that $40,000 build:

A lean year — about $4,000–$6,000

  • Few hundred users, slow growth
  • One phone-update compatibility pass
  • Bugs patched in small monthly batches
  • No new features, no design changes

A busy year — about $8,000–$12,000

  • User base growing, more support emails
  • One compatibility pass plus two store guideline updates
  • One new feature added and bedded in
  • Server upgraded as usage climbed

Both are normal. The failure mode isn't the busy year — it's budgeting for a lean year and then getting one.

What makes maintenance costs jump

Four things move an app from the floor of the band to the ceiling: users (more people, more data, more support), features (payments and logins need care after they ship), age (older code costs more to touch), and change (an app whose business never sits still). A quiet booking app for one salon and a marketplace with thousands of users can share a build budget and still finish $15,000 apart on their yearly upkeep.

Watch the quiet creepers, too. The services behind an app — maps, message sending, identity checks — mostly price by usage, so a fee that was free at launch can start charging once your numbers grow. Each one is small; five of them together is a budget line worth naming.

Maintenance contracts: what to check before signing

Most studios, ours included, run maintenance as a retainer — a fixed monthly amount that buys a set number of hours covering fixes, compatibility work, and small improvements. Contracts differ more in what they leave out, so before signing, check four things:

  1. What's inside and what's extra. New features, redesigns, and security audits are usually quoted separately. Make sure that's written down.
  2. Response times. What happens when the app breaks on a Friday — and what does "urgent" cost?
  3. Who holds the code. You want the contract to say you own it, so any developer can take over.
  4. The exit. Notice period, handover files, and what you walk away with.

One more choice shapes the total: retainer versus pay-as-you-go. Hourly, call-when-broken arrangements look cheaper in a quiet year, but they turn unpredictable the moment something breaks at 7am on a Monday — and unpredictable bills are the kind that wreck budgets. A retainer costs a little more in the quiet months precisely so the loud months don't hurt. Most studios will size one honestly against the app's real activity; ask them to show their working.

We've seen founders pay a "maintenance" fee for years while the contract only covered monitoring and user emails — no actual fix hours. The label means nothing; the inclusions list means everything.

When maintenance isn't worth paying for

Sometimes the honest advice is to stop. If the app is a pilot that never found users, or the business has moved on, paying to keep it on life support is waste.
