The most common mistake with freelance quotes isn't paying too much — it's comparing a freelancer's quote to an agency's and wondering why they're different. You're buying one skilled person's hours, not a team — and that trade has real savings and real risks attached. Freelance app developer cost: the short answer

Rates track experience, and Australia sits at the premium end globally. Before hiring, know the three tiers:

Developer levelTypical AUD hourlyWhat you're getting
Junior (0–2 years)$30–$80Learning on your budget; fine for small jobs with a senior reviewing
Mid-level (2–5 years)$80–$120Ships solid work unsupervised; the sweet spot for most projects
Senior (5+ years)$120–$150+Architecture and judgement; often cheaper overall despite the rate

Offshore freelancers quote $25–$50 an hour and some are excellent, but the saving shrinks with every timezone-delayed question and every misunderstanding a shared office would have caught. Compare total cost of getting it working, not the hourly rate alone.

One sanity check worth running on every freelance quote: divide the total by the mid-level hourly rate and see how many hours of work you're buying — then ask what those hours cover. A $20,000 quote at $100 an hour is 200 hours, which is roughly ten weeks of full-time work. If that sounds small for the app you described, either the scope isn't what you think or the hours aren't there. Good freelancers welcome the question; the rest you learn to walk away from.

Hourly and day rates: what freelancers charge

Most freelancers quote either hourly or per project, and a day rate (roughly 7–8 billable hours) is common for ongoing work. Two things quietly move the number: specialisation — someone who has built apps like yours before charges more and takes less time — and location, with Sydney and Melbourne freelancers typically at the top of the Australian band. Ask every candidate the same two questions: what's your hourly rate, and how many paid hours do you estimate my project needs? The second answer matters more than the first.

What a freelance app project costs end to end

Per-hour figures don't tell you what an app costs, so here's the pattern from real projects: a simple, focused first version — one core job, a handful of screens — typically lands between $15,000 and $40,000 with a competent local freelancer. Add logins, payments, or a backend with an admin panel, and you're into $40,000–$80,000 or beyond. Anything resembling a marketplace or a social platform usually outgrows one person entirely.

That first band overlaps what we'd quote at a studio for the same scope, because the build hours are the same. The difference shows up in what happens around the build — design, testing, project management — which is exactly what the next section is about.

Two more things to settle before you sign. First, quoting model: fixed-price per milestone protects your budget but squeezes out flexibility, while hourly absorbs change without renegotiation but puts the risk of overruns on you. Most freelance projects end up hybrid — fixed price for the known scope, hourly for the changes. Second, the afterlife: the app you buy will still need updates every year — the yearly phone-software pass alone costs real money, and what maintenance runs per month gives you the scale — so ask your freelancer now whether they offer ongoing work, or whether they'll hand over cleanly to whoever comes next.

What you save — and what you take on

The saving is real: no agency overhead, no account managers, no layers. Typically 30–50% less than a local studio for the same build hours. The catch is that a freelancer's quote only covers what one person can do, so some jobs quietly transfer to you:

  • Project management — keeping scope, timeline, and decisions moving is now your job
  • Design — many developers code well but design poorly; you may hire a second freelancer
  • QA and release — testing, app store listings, and review rejections become your problem
  • Continuity — documenting what was built and why, so it survives beyond one person's memory

Hiring a freelancer also makes you a contractor's client under Australian law, which carries its own box of obligations. The government's guide to hiring contractors covers what to get right before any work starts — contracts, insurance, and who's responsible for what.

The risks: one person, one sick week

A freelancer is a single point of failure. Illness, burnout, a full-time job offer, or a busy month can pause your project with no backup — and if you don't hold the code, restarting with someone new means starting from the source code's current state, which you may not have.

I've sat across from two founders in the past year alone who were stuck because their developer "went quiet" mid-build — one got their code, one didn't. Both had paid by milestone without any written agreement about who held what. The fix costs nothing: a short contract that says you own the intellectual property, the code lives in a repository you control from day one, and payments release on milestones you accept. IP Australia's guide to who owns IP explains the default rules — and the defaults often surprise people.

When a freelancer is the right call (and when it isn't)

A freelancer fits when

  • The app has one core job and a small scope
  • You (or someone on your side) can manage the project
  • You have a named, reviewed developer with a track record
  • Budget flexibility matters more than schedule certainty

Think twice when

  • The app needs design, build, and backend skills at once
  • Launch date is fixed and business-critical
  • You can't supervise or test the work yourself
  • The project is really a business platform, not a tool
